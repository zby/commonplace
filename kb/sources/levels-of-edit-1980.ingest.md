# Ingest: The Levels of Edit, Second Edition

## Classification

An operational account of JPL's technical-editing practice, with task specifications and reported management benefits rather than a controlled evaluation. Robert Van Buren and Mary Fran Buehler describe an institutional system used since the first edition in 1976. The second edition revises that system in light of more than three years of experience; this gives the taxonomy a practice basis, not independent evidence of effectiveness.

## Summary

[The Levels of Edit](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19800011701/downloads/19800011701.pdf) defines nine types of editorial work—Coordination, Policy, Integrity, Screening, Copy Clarification, Format, Mechanical Style, Language, and Substantive—and combines them into five cumulative commissioned levels. Level 5 includes Coordination and Policy; Level 1 includes all nine types. The bundles reflect JPL publication classes, composition methods, institutional minimums, and cost constraints, not a prescribed sequence for revising prose. Individual types can also be commissioned separately when institutional requirements permit. Detailed task boundaries distinguish matching publication elements from resolving contradictions in meaning, and language parallelism from conceptual parallelism. The authors report clearer communication, estimates, and accountability, while emphasizing that manuscript condition determines effort within a level. Missing-material research, technical verification, and repeated processing are among the extraordinary functions excluded from normal estimates.

## Connections Found

The source is a counterpoint to reading every editorial hierarchy as an execution order. Compared with [solving low-degree-of-freedom subproblems first](../theory/solve-low-degree-of-freedom-subproblems-first-to-avoid-blocking.md), JPL specifies which services are included, not which decision has the fewest remaining alternatives. Its numerical levels therefore neither establish nor refute that sequencing heuristic.

The [one-improvement procedure proposal](../reference/proposals/one-improvement-procedure-over-atomic-instructions.md) supplies the relevant local contrast: it puts concerns about a note's existence, claim, and boundary before wording because those changes can discard the text being polished. That invalidation rationale belongs to the proposal and this comparison, not to JPL's level numbers. The source supplies task distinctions with which to discuss revision scope, but no test comparing these two ordering mechanisms.

JPL's institutional minimums, author options, editor responsibilities, and separate production responsibilities also compare with [the author–executor decision boundary](../theory/fix-what-the-executor-cant-determine-not-what-it-will.md). They illustrate why deciding who may commission or perform work is separate from deciding when to perform it. They do not establish the note's rule for allocating choices by available evidence.

## Extractable Value

1. **Separate invalidation order from option-count order.** For the caller's question, three different axes must remain distinct: JPL's commissioned scope, the proposal's avoidance of work on text that may be discarded, and the theory note's preservation of scarce feasible options. In our comparison, a revision with many possible outcomes can still invalidate extensive wording work; a decision with few alternatives need not invalidate it. The mechanisms can therefore recommend different orders. This is a target-side distinction, not a sequencing result demonstrated by JPL. [quick-win]

2. **Separate detection scope from repair authority.** The Language Edit discussion explicitly allows a language-level defect to require substantive repair. Integrity checks likewise establish that referenced elements exist without resolving contradictory meanings. For Commonplace, a check can detect a problem outside the repair operation's permitted scope; escalation need not mean that the check was misclassified. This is a reusable boundary illustrated by the source's editorial tasks. [quick-win]

3. **Specify included work without treating the scope label as an effort estimate.** JPL distinguishes the chosen level from manuscript condition and lists extraordinary services requiring revised estimates. This offers a commissioning vocabulary for revision runs: state what is included, what input condition is assumed, and which discoveries require a new commission. The 1980 production bundles themselves are context-bound rather than ready-made Commonplace defaults. [just-a-reference]

## Limitations (our opinion)

The reported improvements in communication, cost estimation, and scheduling are practitioner judgments. The report supplies no measured before/after comparison, independent replication, or comparison of alternative task decompositions. Its three-year revision of the taxonomy shows adaptation of local practice, not that these nine categories or five bundles are optimal.

The bundles depend on JPL's publication classes, production department, institutional policy, and composition technology. The authors acknowledge that some distinctions are arbitrary and that substantive thought enters other edit types. Treat the categories as explicit service boundaries, not disjoint cognitive processes or universal stages. Even a full substantive edit does not include all technical verification: that work appears among extraordinary functions.

Most importantly for reuse, the source does not compare substantive-first revision with language-first revision or with ordering by remaining alternatives. It cannot establish the best order for Commonplace.

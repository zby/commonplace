# Ingest: Intelligent Agents: Theory and Practice (Wooldridge and Jennings, 1995)

## Classification

A survey paper submitted to the Knowledge Engineering Review (October 1994, revised January 1995). It organizes the field into agent theories, agent architectures and agent languages, then reviews applications. It reports and classifies other work; it contains no new formal results or experiments. The authors state that they write from an AI viewpoint and exclude the social, multi-agent "macro" level. Author: Michael Wooldridge and Nicholas R. Jennings, both central figures in multi-agent systems research; the paper adapts Wooldridge's 1992 PhD thesis and became a standard citation for the weak and strong notions of agency.

## Summary

The paper first separates a weak notion of agency (autonomy, social ability, reactivity, pro-activeness) from a stronger AI notion that describes or builds agents with mentalistic concepts such as belief, desire and intention. It defends the intentional stance as an abstraction tool, used when a system's simplest consistent description needs it. Section 2 surveys logics for these attitudes: possible-worlds semantics and the logical omniscience problem, Konolige's deduction model, Cohen and Levesque's intention, and Rao and Georgeff's BDI logics. Section 3 is the part most often reused. It defines a deliberative agent or architecture as one with an explicitly represented, symbolic world model whose decisions are made by logical or pseudo-logical reasoning based on pattern matching and symbol manipulation. It names two problems for that approach: the transduction problem (turning the world into an accurate symbolic description in time) and the representation/reasoning problem (representing complex entities and reasoning with them in time). It adds undecidability and intractability, which defeat any guarantee of useful results within a fixed time bound. It defines a reactive architecture negatively, as one with no central symbolic world model and no complex symbolic reasoning, and describes hybrids as layered combinations in which a reactive subsystem often takes precedence over a deliberative one. The discussion says deliberative systems dominate because they have a development methodology and underlying theory. It says reactive systems are hand-crafted by experiment and that hybrids lack a clean, well-motivated control framework. It lists goal generation and the management of conflicting goals and intentions as open problems. The paper gives no decision-theoretic or utility-based account of action selection, and it contains no account of a deliberative agent learning or revising its own model.

## Contribution assessment (our opinion)

The problem is foundational for its period: a field using "agent" loosely needed shared distinctions before results could be compared. The paper adds organization rather than new theory. It splits the field into theory, architecture and language, treats agent theories as specifications, and gives compact working definitions of weak versus strong agency and of deliberative versus reactive architectures. Its critical discussion sections name real gaps: logical omniscience against resource-bounded agents, the unformalized relation between BDI logics and architectures such as PRS, the missing methodology for reactive systems, and goal generation. Its demonstrated value is as a stable taxonomy and citation anchor; its lasting significance, judged from background knowledge rather than from the capture, is high in the multi-agent-systems literature. The definitions are stipulative ("for our purposes") and are not tested against rival classifications. The survey omits decision-theoretic agents, so its taxonomy is narrower than "agent architectures" in general. These limits narrow the contribution; they do not undermine it, because the paper claims only to be a survey with tentative remarks.

## Connections Found

For the occasion, the source serves as the **definitional anchor** for "deliberative agent". The term has a precise classical sense here: an architectural property, an explicit symbolic world model plus logical or pseudo-logical decision-making. It does not name a choice standard such as utility, and it does not include learning. A term that borrows "deliberative" inherits that architectural sense unless it distinguishes itself.

For the general KB, it is the **classical counterpart** to Commonplace's working agent definition. ["Agent" is a useful technical convention, not a definition](../theory/agent-is-a-tool-loop.md) asserts that "agent" carries too much philosophical weight to define cleanly; this survey is the documented debate behind that claim (compares-with: how to define "agent"). It qualifies the note's claim rather than corroborating it, because the survey does offer usable property-based definitions. Its single autonomy property ("operate without the direct intervention of humans", with control over actions and internal state) compares with the separate allocation, warrant and power dimensions in [Tool usefulness, computational autonomy, warrant, and system power are separate dimensions](../theory/usefulness-autonomy-warrant-and-power-are-separate-dimensions.md). McCarthy's criterion for ascribing mental qualities to machines, quoted in section 2.a, compares with the naive-anthropomorphism and naive-mechanism-ism failure modes in [Human-LLM differences are load-bearing for knowledge system design](../theory/human-llm-differences-are-load-bearing-for-knowledge-system-design.md). The weak notion assumes an agent that encapsulates persistent state, which [Agent statelessness makes routing architectural, not learned](../theory/agent-statelessness-makes-routing-architectural-not-learned.md) shows LLM agents lack across sessions.

Ferguson's three preconditions for purely reactive control, quoted in section 3.d, are a classical instance of fixed, precompiled behaviour that is reliable only when the task fits. That compares with [Constraining and extraction can trade generality for reliability, speed, or cost](../theory/constraining-and-extraction-both-trade-generality-for-reliability.md). It is a quotation of Ferguson, not an independent result.

On resource-bounded reasoning, the survey states the problem and leaves it open. [Principles of Metareasoning (Russell and Wefald, 1991)](./russell-wefald-principles-of-metareasoning.ingest.md), which the survey cites, records the decision-theoretic response. [Provably Bounded-Optimal Agents](./provably-bounded-optimal-agents.ingest.md) records a contemporaneous formal response. The Maes and Kaelbling definitions of an agent architecture as modules plus data and control flow compare with the modern harness decomposition in [Agent Harness for Large Language Model Agents](./agent-harness-large-language-model-agents-survey.ingest.md).

## Extractable Value

1. **The deliberative-agent definition is architectural, not about the choice standard** -- Section 3.a defines a deliberative agent or architecture by two features: an explicitly represented, symbolic world model, and decisions made via logical or pseudo-logical reasoning based on pattern matching and symbolic manipulation. It traces the term to Genesereth's "deliberate agent" and McCarthy's "sentential processing automaton". The draft's comparison can cite this exact definition. It should not attribute to "deliberative agent" any claim about goal tests, utilities or who sets them; the definition is silent on all three. [quick-win]
2. **The contrast classes are defined by subtraction and combination** -- A reactive architecture has no central symbolic world model and no complex symbolic reasoning (3.b). A hybrid combines a deliberative subsystem that plans with a symbolic model and a reactive subsystem that responds without complex reasoning. The reactive layer often has precedence, and the layers form an abstraction hierarchy under a control framework (3.c). For the draft, "deliberative" therefore contrasts with "reactive" along the axis of explicit model and reasoning, not along a learning axis. [quick-win]
3. **What decides action: goals are given, and the survey gives no utility account** -- Planning agents take a symbolic goal state and search for actions that achieve it (STRIPS, 3.a). IRMA filters options for consistency with current intentions, then a "deliberation process" chooses between competing options without a stated criterion. The paper never mentions utility or decision theory. The "rationality" attribute is only acting to achieve one's goals insofar as beliefs permit (1.a). The survey says most AI work assumes one well-defined goal and calls the agent's generation of its own goals largely unaddressed (3.d). It does not state that the designer fixes the standard; the supplied-goal assumption is the nearest statement. Ferguson's "fixed, pre-compiled ranking scheme" describes reactive, not deliberative, systems. A draft claim that deliberative agents have a designer-fixed evaluation standard would be an inference, so it should be labeled as one. [quick-win]
4. **Named problems of the symbolic approach** -- The transduction problem, the representation/reasoning problem, undecidability of first-order and modal logics, and the intractability of symbol manipulation. Together these make time-bounded useful results hard to guarantee. Chapman's results show planning unusable in time-constrained systems (3.a). Logical omniscience makes possible-worlds models unrealistic for resource-bounded agents (2.c, 2.h). These are the established failure modes a borrowed "deliberative" label may evoke in readers who know the classical term. [just-a-reference]
5. **Learning and model revision are outside the deliberative definition** -- The survey mentions learning only as research prompted by the transduction problem (3.a). Agre's routines, "once learned", are encoded in low-level structure needing only periodic updating (3.b). Machine learning or genetic algorithms are a suggested way around hand-crafting reactive systems (3.d), and the NEWT interface agent learns user preferences from examples and feedback (5). For deliberative architectures the survey describes state updates (PRS's interpreter updates beliefs; HOMER modifies plans during execution and keeps episodic memory), never revision of the world model's structure or of the decision procedure. Intention reconsideration is listed as unaddressed (2.h). So "deliberative learning" has no classical precedent in this source; the paper neither uses nor excludes the combination. [quick-win]
6. **Classical anchor for the KB's agent-definition note** -- The weak and strong notions, and the warning that "agent" risks becoming a "noise" term, supply the uncited debate that `agent-is-a-tool-loop` sidesteps. The tool-loop convention drops autonomy, social ability, pro-activeness and persistent state; the comparison makes those drops explicit. [quick-win]
7. **Agent theories as specifications** -- Section 2.h treats agent logics as specification languages and asks how a specification is refined into an implementation. It reports that BDI logic does not give PRS a formal semantics. This is a classical case of a specification and its implementation lacking a stated correspondence. It is not evidence for Commonplace's [program refinement](../theory/definitions/program-refinement.md) term, because the survey defines no refinement relation. [just-a-reference]

## Limitations (our opinion)

As a survey, the paper's definitions are stipulated "for our purposes" and are not argued against rival definitions or tested for boundary cases. Its deliberative/reactive split is coarse: situated automata compile a declarative, logic-of-knowledge specification into a reactive machine, and PRS is listed as hybrid although it is a BDI system with a symbolic plan library. The categories therefore classify implementations, not decision standards, and a reader cannot infer from "deliberative" how options are evaluated. The survey omits decision-theoretic and utility-based agents except by citation (Russell and Wefald), so its silence on utility is a scope choice, not a claim that deliberative agents lack one. Its silence on learning is likewise a gap in coverage of the 1995 literature, not evidence that deliberative agents cannot learn. The paper reports architectures' evaluations (Tileworld, GRATE*'s electricity domain) only by citation, so it provides no comparative evidence about which architecture works better. The field has moved since 1995; the claim that the deliberative paradigm dominates is time-stamped.

# Ingest: Niklas Luhmann Archive keyword registers

## Classification

This is an official institutional statement because the archive describes the contents and intended scope of the keyword registers it publishes.
Author: Niklas Luhmann-Archiv, the institutional publisher of the collection description.

## Summary

The Archive says its digital keyword-register presentation is still being assembled from indexes that Niklas Luhmann created: the ZK I register contains about 1,250 keywords, while the last of four successive ZK II registers contains about 3,200. Its decision-relevant point is narrower than a general account of Zettelkasten navigation: Luhmann's registers explicitly did not claim to locate every occurrence of each term and instead named relevant entry points into the collection. Read this source for first-party archival support for that bounded historical claim, not for evidence about Maps of Content, practitioner behavior, or retrieval performance.

## Quotes

- **Source extract (verbatim):** Die von Luhmann angelegten Verzeichnisse erfüllen dezidiert keine Ansprüche auf Vollständigkeit hinsichtlich der Fundstellen der jeweiligen Begriffe, sondern benennen nur die für den jeweils aufgelisteten Begriff einschlägigen Einstiegsstellen in die Sammlung.
  - **Source location:** “Schlagwortregister,” opening description

## Limitations (our opinion)

This short institutional page gives the Archive's own description, not scans of the registers, a selection methodology, or an independent historical analysis. The manually pasted observation is point-in-time, and the page says the digital registers are still being assembled. It does not show that the registers were Maps of Content, explain how Luhmann used them, generalize to other Zettelkasten or LYT practitioners, or test the stopping-behavior mechanism in [Indexes lower recall when they suppress retrieval that would find more](../notes/indexes-lower-recall-when-they-suppress-retrieval-that-would-find-more.md). The retained quotation supports the exact non-exhaustiveness and entry-point wording, but broader historical or behavioral claims still require other evidence.

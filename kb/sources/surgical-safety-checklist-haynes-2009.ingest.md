# Ingest: A Surgical Safety Checklist to Reduce Morbidity and Mortality in a Global Population

## Classification

This is a peer-reviewed empirical paper: a prospective, non-randomized before-after intervention study with prespecified outcomes, a power calculation and robustness analyses, published as a special article in the New England Journal of Medicine (2009;360:491-9).
Author: Haynes, Gawande and colleagues for the WHO Safe Surgery Saves Lives Study Group (Harvard School of Public Health with site co-investigators in eight countries). The authors designed the checklist they evaluate, and WHO funded the study; no conflict of interest was reported.

## Summary

Eight hospitals in high-, middle- and low-income settings introduced a 19-item checklist derived from the WHO Guidelines for Safe Surgery. Teams confirm items orally at three fixed junctures: before induction of anesthesia (sign in), before skin incision (time out) and before the patient leaves the operating room (sign out). Each site translated the checklist where needed and "adjusted [it] to fit into the flow of care". Across 3733 patients before and 3955 after, inpatient complications fell from 11.0% to 7.0% and in-hospital deaths from 1.5% to 0.8%. The result held with case-mix adjustment, with direct-observer presence as a covariate, when any one site was removed, and under clustering by site. Performance of all six measured safety processes rose from 34.2% to 56.7% of patients, yet "the omission of individual steps was still frequent". The intervention is a bundle. It added a formal pause for team introductions, briefings and debriefings that no site had used before. It brought identity and site confirmation that was new to most hospitals, and three sites moved antibiotic administration into the operating room. The authors therefore call the mechanism "less clear and most likely multifactorial" and cannot separate a Hawthorne effect. The result is an association between the whole program and outcomes; it does not identify which component did the work.

## Contribution assessment (our opinion)

The problem is important and was then poorly measured outside rich countries: avoidable surgical complications at global scale. The paper's main contribution is showing that a cheap, quickly introduced team procedure was associated with large outcome reductions across very different resource settings. It also reports process adherence beside outcomes, which lets a reader see that the procedure was only partly followed. As a test, it is moderately strong for a field intervention. Site-removal cross-validation, the observer covariate and the argument against secular trends rule out several simple rival explanations. Even so, the before-after design leaves secular, attention and regression effects less constrained than a randomized or stepped-wedge design would. The bundle design also prevents any claim about which element mattered. Our tentative judgment, informed by background knowledge rather than a literature check, is that its significance came from policy impact more than mechanism. Later replications reportedly gave mixed results, which this capture cannot assess. The demonstrated value is the program-level association; the mechanism remains open.

## Connections Found

The source's role for the occasion is a **worked human case of operationalization without function-level automation**. Existing guideline knowledge was turned into a procedure fitted to a defined task (one operation), a defined executor (the surgical team, with roles assigned per item) and site-specific settings. No function was allocated to a machine, and every clinical decision stayed with the humans. The case therefore separates operationalization from automation and from autonomy on real outcome data. ## Extractable Value

1. **Operationalization without automation, with outcome evidence** -- for the occasion, this is the clean case of knowledge made usable by a defined executor for a defined task with zero functions automated and full human decision autonomy retained. It counters any reading that operationalization implies a move toward computation. It also shows that the operationalized procedure did not settle all execution: omissions remained frequent. Operationalization, actual execution and outcome quality must be reported separately, which matches the workshop's separation of these dimensions. Bundle and before-after caveats apply. [quick-win]
2. **Operationalization includes changing the executor's operations and setting** -- the checklist was translated, fitted to each site's flow of care, and sometimes required systems changes. Antibiotic administration moved rooms, and a team-wide pause was created. This supports a task-, executor- and setting-relative account of operationalization: the same knowledge yields different site procedures, and some adaptation acts on available operations rather than on the method. [quick-win]
3. **Fixed activation with interpreted response: a measured partial closure** -- rooting activation in workflow junctures raised all-six-indicator adherence by about two thirds, yet 43.3% of patients still missed at least one of the six. This is a data point for the activation/response split. It does not show that the activation component caused the gain, because briefings, systems changes and observation co-varied. [experiment]
4. **Peer-performed confirmation as a designed feedback function** -- the checklist assigns spoken confirmation to specific roles so that the team knows whether steps were done. This is a human-side design for the cross-operator feedback that Norman says automation lacks, and a candidate pattern for human–LLM arrangements where neither party reliably reports state. It is an analogy only until tested on LLM executors. [experiment]
5. **Worked case for bundle-grain attribution** -- the paper carries out the reporting discipline the experiment-contrast note asks for, and it can serve as that note's external example. [just-a-reference]

## Limitations (our opinion)

The design compares a before period with an after period in the same rooms without concurrent controls. The authors' arguments against secular trends (a study period under a year, an external NSQIP comparison cited by personal communication, and stable case mix) narrow but do not eliminate that rival account. Regression to the mean is not addressed. The Hawthorne contribution is acknowledged as "difficult to disentangle". The observer-presence covariate tests only the data collector, not the awareness that the checklist itself creates, and only 37.5% of cases were directly observed. Most process data came from the clinical teams being evaluated. The intervention is a bundle, so no component, including the checklist form, is identified as the cause, as [an experiment identifies only the contrast it actually runs](../theory/an-experiment-identifies-only-the-contrast-it-actually-runs.md) warns. Only three sites showed significant individual reductions in complications, and the death reduction was significant only in the lower-income group. Introduction, briefings and debriefings were required, but their adherence was not measured. Outcomes were inpatient-only, and follow-up did not test durability, which the authors flag. The sites were selected and had committed leadership and full-time data collectors, so feasibility elsewhere is not shown. For this KB, any transfer to LLM executors is analogy. Human motivation, social accountability and observation effects are plausible mechanisms here with no established LLM counterpart.

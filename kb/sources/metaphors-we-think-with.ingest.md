# Ingest: Metaphors We Think With: The Role of Metaphor in Reasoning

## Classification

Scientific paper (PLoS ONE, open access). Genre justified by the source's form: five controlled experiments with quantitative results, participant samples, coding methodology, and statistical analysis reported in a peer-reviewed venue.

Author: Paul H. Thibodeau and Lera Boroditsky. Credibility signal: published in PLoS ONE (2011) after peer review; funded by NSF; authors affiliated with Stanford University cognitive science community; no declared competing interests.

## Summary

Thibodeau and Boroditsky (2011) report five experiments testing whether metaphorical framing affects how people reason about a complex social problem (crime) and gather information to solve it. Using a fictional city ("Addison") with escalating crime statistics, they contrasted two metaphors: crime as a virus (infecting, plaguing) versus crime as a beast (preying, lurking). The key finding is that even a single metaphorical word in the opening sentence ("Crime is a virus/beast ravaging the city of Addison") shifted participants' proposed solutions by 18-22% toward the metaphoric frame—larger than the opinion difference between Democrats and Republicans (8-9%). The effect was covert: participants consistently cited the crime statistics (identical across conditions) as influential, not the metaphor. Experiment 3 showed the effect requires an integrated metaphor within a knowledge structure, not mere lexical priming. Experiment 4 showed the effect persists when all solution options are explicitly listed, and influences information-foraging choices. Experiment 5 showed the effect depends on early presentation in the narrative—late-presented metaphors had no effect. The paper argues metaphors instantiate "frame-consistent knowledge structures" and invite "structurally-consistent inferences."

## Contribution assessment (our opinion)

This paper contributes a clean empirical demonstration that minimal linguistic framing—a single word—can produce large, covert shifts in how people approach a problem and what information they seek. For the problem of understanding figurative wording's influence on reasoning, the paper provides probably the most directly relevant evidence in the literature: it isolates the manipulation (single noun), measures both solution proposals and information-seeking behavior, tests alternative mechanisms (lexical priming vs. knowledge-structure instantiation), and benchmarks effect size against real-world opinion divides. The contribution to the problem itself is the demonstration that metaphor's influence is not merely rhetorical but cognitive—it changes which knowledge structures become active and which inferences seem natural. The central claim is well-supported by the five-experiment design. The main limitation for KB purposes is that the domain is crime policy, a politically loaded topic whose framing effects may not generalize uniformly to all reasoning contexts; the paper's own discussion acknowledges this by reference to the pervasiveness of metaphor in everyday language, but the empirical scope is one domain. The paper does not test whether the effect transfers across domains or addresses counterfactual framing (what happens when a metaphor is explicitly challenged).

This bears directly on the planned note about figurative wording and reasoning. ## Limitations (our opinion)

The source should not be over-generalized in these ways:

- **Single domain (crime policy).** The experiments use crime in a fictional city as the test domain. The paper's own discussion argues metaphor is pervasive in everyday language and social policy discourse, but the empirical scope is one domain. Whether the effect magnitude transfers to other reasoning contexts (technical problem-solving, scientific reasoning, everyday decisions) is not tested. The covert-effect and knowledge-structure claims are plausible across domains, but the specific numbers are domain-bound.

- **Framing, not figurative wording broadly.** The paper contrasts two specific conceptual metaphors (virus vs. beast) that invite different knowledge structures. It does not test other forms of figurative language (metonymy, irony, personification) or non-metaphorical figurative wording. Claims about "figurative wording" more broadly than conceptual metaphor framing exceed what this source establishes.

- **Covert effect is demonstrated, not explained at the cognitive-architecture level.** The paper shows the effect is covert (participants don't recognize it) and argues it operates through frame-consistent knowledge structures, but does not provide a detailed cognitive model of how those structures are built, maintained, or interfered with. The mechanism remains at the level of "analogical transfer" and "elaborated knowledge structures"—useful but not architecturally precise.

- **Not an LLM study.** The paper says nothing about language models. Any KB claim that transfers these findings to LLM behavior (e.g., "LLMs show covert framing effects like humans") would be extrapolation beyond the source, not evidence from it. The human-writing-structures-transfer note's argument about failure-mode overlap is supported by this paper only insofar as it establishes that humans have these framing vulnerabilities; the LLM-side claim requires separate evidence.

- **Capture boundary: full-source.** The snapshot is a full-source capture, so the limitation is not from incomplete capture. The limitation is inherent to the paper's scope.

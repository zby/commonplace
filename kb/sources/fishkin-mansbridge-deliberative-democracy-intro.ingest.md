# Ingest: Introduction to Deliberative Democracy (Fishkin and Mansbridge, Daedalus 2017)

## Classification

This is the editors' introduction to a themed issue of *Dædalus* (146(3),
Summer 2017). It frames a position and previews the issue's essays; it reports
no study of its own. The cited empirical findings belong to the contributors'
essays, which were not captured. The genre is therefore a conceptual essay,
not a scientific paper.
Author: James S. Fishkin (Stanford, Center for Deliberative Democracy, the
originator of Deliberative Polling) and Jane Mansbridge (Harvard Kennedy School,
co-editor of *Deliberative Systems*). Both are leading theorists of deliberative
democracy and declare themselves "deliberative democrats", so the framing is
an advocate's framing that deliberately includes critics.

## Summary

The introduction presents deliberative democracy as a response to distrust of
elites, populism and the "manufactured will" of Schumpeterian competitive
democracy. Its working definition is a "root notion" shared across the issue's
varying definitions: deliberation requires "weighing" competing arguments for
policies or candidates in mutually civil and diverse discussion, where people
can decide on the merits of arguments with good information. Most practical
effort goes into mini-publics: randomly sampled citizens deliberate in
organized settings with balanced briefing materials and competing experts,
after which their "considered judgments" are harvested. Deliberative Polling
measures opinions at recruitment and again after a weekend of deliberation.
The rationale is counterfactual: a representative sample deliberating under
good conditions shows what the whole population would think under those
conditions. The authors then preview criticisms: inequality among participants
(Lupia and Norton), "blind deference" to mini-publics by citizens who did not
deliberate (Lafont), and the realism and consensus objections of Shapiro's
competitive model. They also preview defences: Siu on inequality, Bächtiger
and Beste on justification quality, and Deliberative Polls in Uganda. The
piece is short, and each essay gets one or two sentences.

## Connections Found

For the occasion, the source serves as the **definitional anchor** for the
group sense of deliberation: weighing competing arguments in discussion under
stated conditions. In the KB, it lands mainly against
[On-balance judgments can guide improvement without a global measure](../theory/necessary-preference-can-guide-improvement-without-a-global-measure.md).
Both treat a good judgment as reasons weighed rather than a single score. This
source adds the group-level conditions under which the weighing is
trusted: good information, balanced materials, diverse participants and
competing experts. Lafont's objection parallels that note's "whose costs
count" question. A citizen who did not deliberate cannot tell whether the
majority's weighing would have been hers. The factual inputs can be checked,
but the weighing cannot simply be delegated.

Two source-to-source comparisons fix what "deliberative" means.
[Wooldridge and Jennings](./wooldridge-jennings-intelligent-agents.ingest.md)
use "deliberative" for an agent architecture with a symbolic world model and
logical action selection. That sense involves no weighing of competing
arguments among people, so it is a different term and not a precursor of this
one. [Making reflective equilibrium precise](./making-reflective-equilibrium-precise.ingest.md)
models weighing as a fixed weighted sum. Fishkin and Mansbridge treat it as a
discursive process whose conditions decide which arguments are heard. The two
sources mark the formal and discursive ends of one question.

As a secondary **counterpoint**, the source bears on
[Decorrelated reviewers still share the field's prior](../theory/reviewers-share-the-fields-prior-so-interpret-findings-by-stance.md).
A mini-public samples at random in order to reproduce the population's view
under good conditions, so a shared prior is the goal. Review gates use several
judges to catch errors, and there a shared prior is the defect. The phrase
"diverse judges" thus serves opposite aims. As an **analogy only**, the
manufactured-will argument ("the normative causal arrow goes in the wrong
direction") has the same structure as the non-antecedent objective in
[Self-improvement is relative to a declared objective](../theory/self-improvement-is-relative-to-a-declared-objective.md):
the criterion is produced by the process it is meant to legitimize. This
parallel comes from politics and is not evidence for the note.

## Extractable Value

1. **Definition of deliberation, occasion item 1** -- The authors give a
   "root notion" shared across the issue's varying definitions, not one
   canonical definition. Deliberation requires "weighing" competing arguments
   for policies or candidates in mutually civil and diverse discussion, where
   people decide on the merits with good information. "Weighing" appears in
   scare quotes and is not analysed further. Bernard Manin's essay offers a
   rival core, the "adversarial principle" ("confrontation of opposing
   positions"). A definition citing this source should say "root notion" and
   should not present it as settled consensus. [quick-win]
2. **Conditions attached, occasion item 2** -- Good information, civility,
   diversity of discussants and decision on the merits are part of the root
   notion. Balance is a design condition of mini-publics, not of the
   definition: briefing materials "make the best case for and against", and
   participants question competing experts. Giving reasons is not in the
   definition. It enters only as a quality measure in contributors' work:
   Bächtiger and Beste's Discourse Quality Index asks whether deliberators
   justify assertions, and Siu counts "justified" arguments. The authors also
   say much depends on "the account of good conditions", so the conditions are
   open to dispute rather than fixed. [quick-win]
3. **Is a group required? Occasion item 3** -- The definition is phrased as
   discussion among people, and every example is collective: legislatures,
   executive-branch policy teams, public forums and mini-publics. The authors
   never consider a single person deliberating, so they neither require nor
   exclude it. A definition that needs the group sense can rest on this
   source. A claim that deliberation is *necessarily* social goes beyond it and
   needs the individual sense from a practical-reason source as its contrast.
   [quick-win]
4. **What deliberation produces and what outlasts the participants, occasion
   item 4** -- Named products are "considered judgments", post-deliberation
   opinions measured against pre-deliberation ones, agreed statements or
   verdicts from citizens' juries and consensus conferences, recommendations to
   the public or policy-makers, and some binding decisions. The authors claim
   that post-deliberation views are "more evidence-based and reflective of the
   merits". They imply opinion change through the before-and-after design but
   report no effect sizes here. They do not call any of this "learning".
   Results reach non-participants only as recorded artifacts: poll data,
   recordings, statements, recommendations and decisions. Their authority is
   counterfactual (what the population *would* think), and Lafont disputes it.
   The introduction says nothing about whether participants keep their changed
   views over time. A "deliberative learning" definition can cite the source
   for changed judgments and recorded outputs, but not for learning or
   retention. [quick-win]
5. **Criticisms and failure modes, occasion item 5** -- The authors name:
   inequality among participants, where advantaged participants dominate and
   outcomes reproduce power relations ("inequality is always in the room");
   unrepresentative samples or poor conditions; "blind deference" by
   non-participants who cannot know whether they would have joined the
   majority; the lack of realism of the deliberative model; veto power for
   intense minorities when consensus is the goal; "the standard argument that
   politicians do not want to deliberate and citizens are not able to"; an
   information environment of fake news, narrow-casting, filter bubbles and
   like-minded discussion; and use of deliberation to legitimate existing power
   ("deliberative authoritarianism"). Most of these are failures of the group
   conditions, not of weighing as such. A KB definition can carry them as the
   conditions whose absence voids the label. [quick-win]
6. **Representation versus error detection as aims for a group of judges** --
   The mini-public rationale makes a shared population prior a goal, while
   review gates treat it as a defect. This could become a theory claim that
   *what a judging group is for decides whether shared priors are a feature or
   a defect*. The introduction states the representation side too briefly to
   carry it alone. [deep-dive]
7. **Disambiguating "deliberative"** -- The group-weighing sense here differs
   from the agent-architecture sense in Wooldridge and Jennings. Any
   Commonplace term built on "deliberative" should say which sense it uses.
   [quick-win]

## Limitations (our opinion)

This is an advocate's framing of a symposium. The definition is a
consensus "root notion" offered to unify varied essays, not an argued analysis
of what weighing is. The text does not explain how competing arguments are
weighed, what makes one weighing better than another, or how civility and
diversity bear on the result. The definition names deliberation but does not
explain its mechanism. Every empirical claim (Siu on low distortion,
Bächtiger and Beste on parliamentary deliberation, the Ugandan Deliberative
Polls, the claim that post-deliberation views are more evidence-based) comes
from uncaptured essays and appears here in one sentence, often with the
authors' interpretation. The ingest therefore cannot establish the size or
robustness of opinion change, or whether inequality distorts outcomes; the
authors themselves call the latter an open empirical question. The authors
cite their own method (Deliberative Polling) and naturally favour it. The
group setting is assumed rather than argued, so the source cannot show that
deliberation is necessarily social. The capture is full text, but pdftotext
interleaved the two-column layout with running heads and marginal text. A few
passages, including the page containing the definition, need care when read
or quoted.

# Ingest: A Glimpse of Truth Maintenance (Doyle, IJCAI-79)

## Classification

A short conference paper (IJCAI-79, pp. 232-237) that presents a system design with worked examples and no empirical evaluation. It is the condensed version of MIT AI Memo 521, "A Truth Maintenance System", which the paper says treats the foundations and applications in much more depth. Author: Jon Doyle, then at the MIT AI Laboratory; this line of work became the standard justification-based truth maintenance system (JTMS) of the AI literature. The capture is uncorrected OCR of a scanned PDF. The analysis below was checked against the page images for the passages it relies on: the abstract and introduction, the dependency-directed backtracking steps, and the meeting-room example.

## Summary

The Truth Maintenance System (TMS) is a subsystem that sits beside a problem solver and records why each belief is held. Beliefs are *nodes*; reasons are *justifications*. A support-list (SL) justification names an *inlist* of nodes that must be believed and an *outlist* of nodes that must not be believed. A conditional-proof (CP) justification records a hypothetical argument. A node is *in* when at least one of its justifications is valid and *out* otherwise. Doyle stresses that in/out is not true/false: it describes whether valid reasons exist, not the statement's truth value. A node whose supporting justification has a non-empty outlist is an *assumption*: it is believed because some other node is not believed. This is how defaults work. Whenever the problem solver adds a justification, *truth maintenance* recomputes non-circular "well-founded support" and updates every node whose support depended on a changed belief. Inconsistency is not built in. The problem solver must mark a node as a *contradiction*. When such a node comes in, *dependency-directed backtracking* traces its well-founded support to the maximal assumptions behind it. It then records a *nogood* node, stating that this set of assumptions is inconsistent, which persists after the conflict is resolved. Finally, it retracts one assumption (the culprit) by justifying one of the nodes in its outlist. Because the culprit choice may be wrong, that retracting justification names the other assumptions, so a later contradiction can shift the blame. A worked scheduling example shows defaults, a first retraction, and a second retraction shaped by the first nogood.

## Contribution assessment (our opinion)

The problem is central to any reasoner that must act on incomplete information: how to adopt beliefs without full grounds and withdraw them cleanly when they fail. Our informed guess is that the paper's lasting contributions are three. First, non-monotonic justifications make "believed because something else is not believed" an explicit, inspectable reason. Second, in/out is cleanly separated from truth. Third, dependency-directed backtracking (the paper cites Stallman and Sussman's use of it for the efficiency evidence) is packaged as a general-purpose subsystem that records a nogood and makes its own blame assignment revisable. Background knowledge says the design was highly influential: de Kleer's ATMS and much later belief-revision and constraint-learning work were defined against it. That is historical reception, not something this capture establishes. Within this short version the support is design argument and small worked examples. There is no evaluation, no complexity analysis, and no formal semantics; the paper defers the last to McDermott and Doyle's non-monotonic logic and the efficiency evidence to Stallman and Sussman. These gaps narrow what the short paper demonstrates but do not undermine the central contribution, which is a mechanism specified clearly enough to implement.

## Connections Found

For the occasion, the source serves as a **mechanized boundary case** for "open to criticism". It shows exactly what recorded reasons buy: belief withdrawal that is automatic, local, and reversible. It also shows what they leave out. Only assumptions can be blamed. Revision starts only when new support arrives or the problem solver signals a contradiction. Nothing in the TMS judges which belief deserves to be held.

Against the KB's existing cases, the closest relative is the [ATMS ingest](./an-assumption-based-tms.ingest.md). The two compare on how support is represented. Doyle keeps one current context and must retract and backtrack. De Kleer labels each datum with its assumption environments, so incompatible contexts coexist. Both keep support validity separate from warrant, and Doyle states that boundary explicitly as in/out versus true/false. The [base contraction ingest](./in-defense-of-base-contraction.ingest.md) compares on dependency-preserving belief change. Hansson argues at the representation level what the TMS mechanizes, while the TMS adds outlist (non-monotonic) support. The [Bacchus and van Run ingest](./bacchus-van-run-dynamic-variable-ordering-csps.ingest.md) compares on non-chronological backtracking: Doyle's procedure is an ancestor of the conflict-directed backjumping whose gain that paper finds can vanish under a strong variable-ordering heuristic.

For theory, the source is evidence that bounds three notes. The [cost-sensitive formalisms note](../theory/cost-sensitive-formalisms-for-tentative-theory-search.md) says a fallible analogue of conflict-directed search must permit later retraction of learned constraints. Doyle's nogood is itself logically sound, but his culprit retraction is built to be overturned: it names the other suspects because "the backtracker may be mistaken in its assignment of blame". That is a concrete design for fallible blame, and it gives the conflict-directed family the source grounding its Scope asks for. The nogood also instantiates [a failure explanation that changes a later branch decision](../theory/failure-explanation-changes-later-branch-decisions.md): in the worked example the first nogood shapes the second backtrack. TMS defaults are an instance of [provisional search control through backtracking](../theory/backtracking-keeps-lightweight-search-control-provisional.md), because an assumption's support is the absence of belief in its rivals.

By comparison, the KB's [make-like staleness detection](../theory/link-graph-plus-timestamps-enables-make-like-staleness-detection.md) flags notes for review from links and timestamps. The TMS propagates change through justifications with exact validity conditions. The contrast shows that KB links can flag a dependent for review but cannot decide whether it still holds. The same precondition, every belief stated as a node with explicit reasons, separates the TMS from the memory systems in [how corrections propagate through retained memory](../agentic-systems/comparisons/memory/correction-propagation.md), which propagate correction only along page-level routes. On what survives displacement, a retracted assumption stays as a node ready to come back in, and the nogood is kept on purpose. This is a mechanized case for [superseded choices keeping a historical witness](../theory/superseded-choices-are-retained-superseded-beliefs-are-not.md). The retained records have operational standing, not subject-matter standing. The [lineage](../theory/definitions/lineage.md) definition's purposes (invalidation, review, selective rollback) are served here at the level of single beliefs rather than artifacts.

## Learning Claims (our opinion)

**Mechanism on its own terms.** The TMS does not learn rules or improve its inference. It keeps a belief set consistent with recorded reasons as reasons are added. The closest thing to retained learning is the nogood: a CP-justified record that a set of assumptions is jointly inconsistent. The record "will be remembered even after the contradiction has been resolved", and Doyle says the summarization "keeps the system from making the same mistake in the future". The paper attributes efficiency gains from this and from non-chronological backtracking to Stallman and Sussman and gives no measurement of its own.

**Answers to the occasion's questions.**

- *How recorded reasons allow withdrawal.* A belief is in only while one of its justifications is valid. Withdrawal is therefore not deletion. A justification loses validity, and truth maintenance propagates the change to every node whose well-founded support depended on it. The withdrawn node and its justifications remain and come back in if support returns.
- *What triggers revision.* Not only contradiction. Any newly added justification can change the belief set: a valid reason for a rival alternative makes a default go out with no contradiction at all. Contradiction-driven revision requires the problem solver to mark a node as a contradiction, because the TMS has "no built-in notion of inconsistency". Any node may be marked, so "contradiction" means whatever the problem solver declares to be unacceptable.
- *Who starts reconsideration.* Once a contradiction node comes in, the TMS acts on its own. It traces support, records the nogood, and retracts a culprit, and it repeats backtracking while the contradiction stays in. But it never begins by doubting a belief that no new input bears on. The problem solver supplies every justification and every contradiction marking. The extensible default structure even "requires an external mechanism" to propose a replacement default. In theory-builder terms, the TMS performs blame assignment and retraction but not noticing or conjecture.
- *Choosing what to believe.* The TMS has no criterion of truth or merit. It believes whatever has a currently valid reason, and an assumption's outlist states "the specific incompletenesses of knowledge authorizing the assumption". Which default to adopt is fixed by how the problem solver writes the justifications. Which assumption to blame is restricted to the "maximal" assumptions, but within that set the worked example chooses "arbitrarily". The fallibility of that choice is handled by keeping the blame revisable, not by choosing well.

**Theory-builder conditions, taking the TMS plus its problem solver as the declared system.**

1. *Localized content*: strong. Every belief and every reason is a separate node or justification, which is a very high grade of [addressability](../theory/definitions/addressable-theory.md).
2. *Consumption*: asserted, not shown here. The abstract says recorded reasons guide the problem solver's actions; this short paper does not demonstrate it.
3. *Criticism*: partial. Blame is assigned structurally through support records, and the TMS treats node statements as opaque. Content-directed criticism happens only in the problem solver's rules, which detect contradictions. Only assumptions can be blamed. Premises, monotonic deductions, and the rules themselves (rule R-37 in the example) are never revised, and backtracking simply halts "when no assumptions can be found underlying the contradiction". The blame assignment is itself open to revision, which matches the definition's point that a criticism can be wrong.
4. *Iteration*: met within a run. The nogood and the retracting justification shape the next backtrack, as the second step of the scheduling example shows.

Persistence is within one problem-solving run; the paper claims nothing across problems. Learning in Simon's sense (improved capacity for future action) is claimed only as avoiding repeated mistakes, with the evidence delegated to another paper.

**What it adds or puts in question.** For "open to criticism", the TMS separates two things the condition could blur. One is *withdrawability*, achieved mechanically by recorded reasons. The other is *openness to criticism*, which also needs someone to raise the objection and a way to blame the rules and premises. The TMS has the first in full and the second only for beliefs that were marked as assumptions at entry. Its effective update space is the in/out status of nodes. The node vocabulary, the justifications, the set of alternatives for a default, and the contradiction markings are all fixed outside it, which is the situation described in [learning inside a fixed decomposition inherits its mistakes](../theory/learning-inside-a-fixed-decomposition-inherits-its-mistakes.md). This is our interpretation, not Doyle's framing.

## Extractable Value

1. **Withdrawability is not openness to criticism** -- In the TMS, recorded reasons make withdrawal automatic and reversible. But only beliefs entered as assumptions can be blamed, and revision waits for a new reason or a contradiction that the problem solver signals. A condition stating "open to criticism" can use this to say what reason-recording supplies and what it needs beyond that: an agent that raises objections, and reach over premises and rules as well as assumptions. [quick-win]
2. **Revision has two triggers, not one** -- New support for a rival withdraws a default without any contradiction. Contradiction-driven revision works on whatever node the problem solver marks as unacceptable. This gives the occasion a precise answer to "contradiction only, or more" and a vocabulary for triggers. [quick-win]
3. **Fallible blame made revisable** -- The culprit is retracted through a justification that names the other suspects, so a later contradiction can move the blame. This is a concrete design for the "fallible analogue" the cost-sensitive formalisms note asks for, and a pattern for retracting a KB claim while keeping the alternatives that were not blamed. [experiment]
4. **In/out versus true/false** -- Doyle states explicitly that in/out records whether valid reasons exist, independent of truth. This is the earliest and clearest source for the "conditional support, not warrant" boundary that the ATMS ingest recommends writing up, giving that note a second, justification-based case alongside the ATMS. [quick-win]
5. **The outlist as a declared incompleteness** -- An assumption's outlist states which missing knowledge licenses it. A KB claim could likewise state what it is assuming is absent, which tells a reviewer what new evidence would defeat it. This is a transfer conjecture of ours; the source does not suggest it. [deep-dive]
6. **Nogoods as retained failure explanations** -- A sound record of which assumptions are jointly inconsistent persists after the conflict is resolved and changes a later branch decision. This is a precise non-LLM instance for the failure-explanation note, though one that limits rather than corroborates its claim about fallible natural-language explanations. [just-a-reference]

## Limitations (our opinion)

This is a short paper with worked examples, not an evaluation. Its efficiency claim rests on Stallman and Sussman, and its semantics on a separate paper, so it establishes a mechanism, not its performance or correctness. The design assumes the problem solver supplies correct justifications and marks contradictions correctly. A missing contradiction marking leaves a bad belief in place, and a wrong justification is never itself blamed. The paper gives no culprit-selection policy beyond maximality, so it says nothing about choosing well, only about recovering from bad choices. Background knowledge suggests later work found difficulties with well-founded support under circular non-monotonic dependencies, along with the cost of single-context backtracking (the ATMS's motivation). This short version does not discuss either, so its silence should not be read as a solution. The capture is uncorrected OCR: several justification formulas (the default-structure forms in section 3, and in this text form (1)/(3) in section 4) are garbled or missing. Claims relying on exact notation need the page images or the full memo. Finally, the system is symbolic and single-agent, with beliefs as opaque statements. Transferring its mechanisms to natural-language KB claims, whose dependencies are rarely stated as exact validity conditions, is our analogy and has not been tested.

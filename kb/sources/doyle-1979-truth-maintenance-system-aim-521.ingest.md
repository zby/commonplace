# Ingest: A Truth Maintenance System (Doyle, MIT AI Memo 521, 1979)

## Classification

A technical memo (MIT AI Memo 521, June 1979) presenting a system design with worked examples, algorithms in prose, and a design rationale, but no empirical evaluation. It is the full version of the IJCAI-79 paper "A Glimpse of Truth Maintenance". It is based on Doyle's 1977 master's thesis, and its acknowledgements say Sections 6 (dialectical arguments) and 7 (models of others' beliefs) and the Section 1.1 view of reasoning are new to this memo. Author: Jon Doyle, then at the MIT AI Laboratory; this is the founding document of justification-based truth maintenance (JTMS). The capture is uncorrected OCR of an image-only scan. The analysis below was checked against the page images for the passages it relies on: the abstract, Section 1.1 (memo pp. 4–6), the Step 5 relaxation comment (p. 18), the backtracking steps (p. 21), Section 6 (pp. 25–27), the Section 9 extensions (p. 36), the opening of Section 8 (pp. 29–30), and the Section 10 discussion (p. 37).

## Summary

The memo gives the full account of the Truth Maintenance System (TMS) whose core mechanism the short IJCAI paper condenses. Beliefs are nodes. Reasons are justifications. A node is *in* while at least one justification is valid and *out* otherwise. An outlist makes a node an *assumption*: it holds because some other node is not believed. The memo adds three things the short paper lacks. First, Section 1.1 states a theory of reasoning: "rational thought is the process of finding reasons for attitudes" (belief, desire, intent, action), and attitudes "have no independent existence" apart from the current set of reasons. It introduces *reasoned retraction*: an assumption is retracted only by giving a reason, so the assumption comes back if that reason later fails. Second, it gives the full algorithms: well-founded support, the three kinds of circular argument, a relaxation step that Doyle admits "must be fairly blind in choosing what revision to make", and dependency-directed backtracking with legible formulas for the nogood and the culprit-retracting justification. Third, Sections 5–8 give applications. Section 5 summarizes explanations into levels of detail through conditional-proof justifications. Section 6 organizes the problem solver as dialectically arguing modules. Each argument step is reified as a node the TMS assumes unchallenged. Premise justifications become assumptions too, and arguments are never discarded. Section 7 models other agents' belief systems with the same mechanism. Section 8 treats choosing among belief revisions as a control problem, handled by "canned" control patterns (defaults, ordered alternatives, equivalence-class representatives) and by explicit choice rules, which are sketched as revision plans in a reflexive interpreter deferred to Doyle's thesis.

## Contribution assessment (our opinion)

The problem is how a reasoner that must act on incomplete information can adopt beliefs without full grounds and withdraw them cleanly. It is central to AI planning and to any belief-revision account that has to be implemented. Our informed guess is that the memo's lasting contributions go beyond the mechanism the short paper conveys. One is an explicit separation of support status from truth, defended at length (Section 2.1's four-valued belief set; Section 10's "opinion revision system"). Another is reasoned retraction, which makes withdrawal itself reversible. The most original part for the problem of criticism is Section 6. It turns argument steps into defeasible beliefs, so any step or premise can be challenged by an added argument, while the argument store stays additive. Background knowledge says this memo, with its journal version in *Artificial Intelligence* (1979), became the reference JTMS, and that later work (de Kleer's ATMS, formal semantics of non-monotonic reasoning) was defined partly against its circularity and incrementality costs. That is historical reception, not something the capture establishes. Support within the memo is design argument, small worked examples, and a list of systems that used the TMS. There is no measurement, no complexity analysis, and no formal semantics, which the memo defers to McDermott and Doyle's non-monotonic logic. The dialectical and control sections are proposals with one toy example each, and the revision-plan mechanism is promised for a later thesis. These gaps narrow what Sections 6–8 demonstrate. They do not undermine the core mechanism, which is specified clearly enough to implement.

## Connections Found

For the occasion, the memo is the **corrective full statement** behind the [Glimpse ingest](./doyle-1979-glimpse-of-truth-maintenance.ingest.md). The two compare on coverage. The short paper supports reading the TMS as a boundary case: withdrawable beliefs, but criticism limited to assumptions and to signalled contradictions. The memo keeps that reading for the bare TMS. It then shows a usage discipline (Section 6) under which premises and argument steps become challengeable, and it states the open problem the short paper leaves silent: the system needs explicit rules for choosing which revision to make. The memo also gives legible forms (1)–(3) for the nogood and culprit justifications that the Glimpse capture garbles.

Against criticism theory, Section 6 compares with the [Popper EWKS ingest](./popper-epistemology-without-a-knowing-subject-1968.ingest.md) on criticism of objective arguments. Doyle mechanizes it: a challenge targets a stated step or premise, and the challenge is itself an argument whose steps can be challenged in turn. It compares with the [reflective equilibrium ingest](./making-reflective-equilibrium-precise.ingest.md) on choosing which commitments to give up. Doyle cites Quine's view that any belief can be rejected at the cost of other changes and calls the choice "fundamentally pragmatic". He mentions Rescher's "minimum mutilation" framework only as literature and supplies no criterion of his own. The [base contraction ingest](./in-defense-of-base-contraction.ingest.md) compares on what the revisable representation is. In Section 6 the store of arguments only grows and the belief set is derived from it, so nothing is contracted: a belief is defeated by an added challenge. On who starts reconsideration, the [Bratman–Israel–Pollack ingest](./bratman-israel-pollack-1988-plans-resource-bounded-reasoning.ingest.md) is the contrast. Doyle revises intentions as reason-backed attitudes whenever new reasons arrive. IRMA keeps intentions stable and adds an explicit filter-override decision about when to reconsider. The [ATMS ingest](./an-assumption-based-tms.ingest.md) keeps the support-representation comparison from the Glimpse ingest. The memo adds Doyle's own admission that the single-context relaxation step is blind and sometimes non-incremental, and a wish in Section 9 for "multiple supporting-justifications".

For theory, Section 6 is evidence for [explicit retention providing direct targets for selective revision](../theory/only-explicit-retention-is-durable-writable-and-addressable.md). Before steps were reified, Doyle writes, the only way to reject a belief by argument was "physically removing justifications". Reifying each step as a node gives every step a direct revision target, within a symbolic system. Section 1.1 bounds [revision guided by rationale needs faithfulness](../theory/revision-guided-by-rationale-needs-faithfulness-not-just-legibility.md): the TMS's rationale is faithful by construction because the belief state is computed from it. The [cost-sensitive formalisms note](../theory/cost-sensitive-formalisms-for-tentative-theory-search.md) gains Doyle's statement that random culprit choice "relies on blind search" and his two remedies (canned control patterns and explicit choice rules). Section 6's claim that accumulated arguments keep future debates "from repeating past debates" extends the [failure-explanation note](../theory/failure-explanation-changes-later-branch-decisions.md) and the [superseded-choices note](../theory/superseded-choices-are-retained-superseded-beliefs-are-not.md): defeated arguments are retained with operational standing. Sections 6 and 8 are a partial case of a [reflective system](../theory/definitions/reflective-system.md). Argument steps are represented as beliefs, and challenging one changes what is believed. The reflexive interpreter that would represent control state and revision plans is only proposed.

## Learning Claims (our opinion)

**Mechanism on its own terms.** The TMS does not learn rules or improve its inference procedures. It maintains a belief set as a function of recorded reasons, which are added over time. Section 6 changes what can be revised without changing the TMS. A module that wants to justify node N creates a node J stating that the inference step holds, and a node ¬J stating that the step is challenged. J is justified by the absence of ¬J. Rejecting the step means justifying ¬J. Since "premise justifications for nodes now become assumptions", premises are challengeable in the same way. Retained results within a run are nogoods, culprit-retracting justifications, and the growing argument store.

**Answers to the occasion's questions, with corrections to the Glimpse ingest.**

- *How recorded reasons allow withdrawal.* As in the short paper, a node goes out when no justification is valid, and the change propagates to dependents. The memo adds *reasoned retraction*. A retraction is itself a justification (of a node in the assumption's outlist), so if that reason later fails, the retraction lapses and the assumption returns. Withdrawal is never deletion of the belief or of the reasons for it.
- *What triggers revision.* Three triggers, all mechanically the addition of a justification. A rival alternative can gain support, and a default goes out with no contradiction. The problem solver can mark a node as a contradiction, which starts dependency-directed backtracking. And, new in the memo, a debater can add an argument against a step or premise by justifying its "challenged" node. Section 6 states this as the first benefit of the dialectical organization: "we can reject any belief in a uniform fashion, simply by producing a new, as yet unchallenged, argument against some step or premise". Section 8 adds that contradictions from new observations can be resolved in either direction: reject the observation ("hallucinating") or reject the prior belief. "Either of these ways of revising beliefs may be warranted in different circumstances."
- *Who starts reconsideration.* The Glimpse conclusion stands for the TMS itself: it never doubts a belief that no new input bears on. The memo, however, assigns the initiating role inside the system. In the dialectical organization each module is "a debater rather than an inference rule", charged with arguing for its conclusion and against opposing ones. The debating procedure has proponents reply to challenges by challenging the challenger's premises or steps. Section 9 also proposes having the TMS signal the problem solver "whenever the argument for some node changes". So the objection-raising agent that the Glimpse ingest found missing is designed into the memo's architecture. The memo demonstrates it with one three-node example, not a working debate.
- *Can justifications and premises be challenged?* Yes, under the Section 6 discipline. This **corrects** the Glimpse ingest's statement that premises and rules "are never revised". That holds for the bare TMS as used in the short paper; the memo's backtracking commentary confirms the TMS alone "foregoes the ability ... to retract premise justifications". The limit comes from how the problem solver encodes justifications, not from the TMS. Section 1.1 also represents inference rules as beliefs, and rule nodes appear in inlists (R-37 in the example), so a rule could be denied the same way. The memo does not show a rule being challenged. The cost is stated: this organization "makes blind dependency-directed backtracking useless, since the number of assumptions supporting a node becomes very large".
- *Does the argument store only grow?* Yes, by design in Section 6: "The system never discards arguments", and "all changes to beliefs occur by adding new arguments to a monotonically growing store of arguments". The belief set stays non-monotonic. Only the store of arguments is monotonic. (The bare TMS of Section 3.2 does allow justifications to be subtracted; Section 6 makes that unnecessary.)
- *Choosing between alternative belief systems.* The abstract stresses "the need of problem solvers to choose between alternative systems of beliefs". This **corrects** a reading of the short paper as indifferent to choosing well. The memo treats the lack of a choice mechanism as the central open problem. It says relaxation is "fairly blind", random culprit choice is "inadequate for all but the simplest sorts of problems", and Section 6 "entails ... that we must explicitly provide ways to choose what to believe". The criterion is pragmatic, following Quine. Truth is explicitly excluded from the "narrowly psychological rationality" the program operates by. The stated reason the choice matters is practical: what the program believes guides what it does. Doyle names example rules ("Reject the smallest set of beliefs possible to restore consistency", "Reject those beliefs which represent the simplest explanation of the inconsistency"), but as typical conventional rules, not as his criterion. His proposed mechanism is revision plans indexed by problem-solver state (history, control state, domain, last action), each a specialization of dependency-directed backtracking. In practice the problem solver can only approximate the set of possible revisions by "the set of assumptions it can change directly". The mechanism is deferred to a forthcoming thesis.

**Theory-builder conditions, taking the dialectically organized TMS plus its problem solver as the declared system.**

1. *Localized content*: strong. Beliefs, reasons, and under Section 6 the individual argument steps are separate nodes, a very high grade of [addressability](../theory/definitions/addressable-theory.md).
2. *Consumption*: asserted. Section 1.1 and Section 8 make desires, intentions, and the choice of action functions of the current reasons. The memo's examples show beliefs selecting a meeting day or a value to propagate, but nothing shows action guided by a theory's content beyond that.
3. *Criticism*: substantially stronger than the short paper shows, but only as a design. A challenge can target any step or premise, and the challenge can itself be challenged, which matches the definition's point that criticism is stated and criticizable. Content-directed criticism lives in the debater modules, since the TMS treats statements as opaque. Comparison among rivals (choosing which revision) has no mechanism beyond blind search.
4. *Iteration*: met within a run. Nogoods and retained arguments shape later backtracking and keep debates "from repeating past debates".

Persistence is within one problem-solving run; the memo claims nothing across problems. Learning in Simon's sense is not claimed or measured.

**What it adds or puts in question.** The memo sharpens the Glimpse ingest's split between withdrawability and openness to criticism. Withdrawability comes from recorded reasons. Openness to criticism additionally needs three things the memo names: challengeable steps and premises (Section 6), agents that raise challenges (debaters), and explicit rules for choosing among revisions (Section 8). The memo supplies the first two in design and leaves the third open. It also shows a tension relevant to any "open to criticism" condition: widening what can be challenged multiplies assumptions, which makes blind blame assignment useless and puts the burden on choice rules. In the bare TMS the effective update space is the in/out status of nodes, with vocabulary, justifications and choice policy fixed outside it, as in [learning inside a fixed decomposition inherits its mistakes](../theory/learning-inside-a-fixed-decomposition-inherits-its-mistakes.md). The reflexive interpreter would bring rules and control into that space, but it remains a proposal. This assessment is our interpretation, not Doyle's framing.

## Extractable Value

1. **Corrections for condition 3** -- The Glimpse ingest's claims that premises and rules are never revised, and that nothing judges which belief to hold, are true of the bare TMS but not of Doyle's design. Section 6 makes steps and premises challengeable. Sections 6 and 8 call explicitly for pragmatic choice rules. The workshop's boundary case should cite the memo so condition 3 is not argued against a weaker version of the TMS than Doyle proposed. [quick-win]
2. **Challenging a step or premise is a revision trigger distinct from contradiction** -- An added argument against a step defeats a belief with no inconsistency signal. This answers the occasion's "contradiction only, or also a new argument" question in favour of the latter, under the dialectical discipline. [quick-win]
3. **Monotonic argument store, non-monotonic belief set** -- Doyle keeps every argument and derives current belief from the whole store, so revision is always addition. This is a concrete design for a KB in which criticism is appended rather than overwriting, with defeated arguments kept for operational use. It bears on how Commonplace retains criticism alongside claims. [experiment]
4. **Widening criticism raises the cost of choosing a revision** -- Reifying every step makes blind backtracking useless, because the assumption set grows large. Any "open to criticism" condition that broadens what can be challenged needs a selection policy to go with it. Doyle names the requirement but leaves the policy open. [deep-dive]
5. **Pragmatic, not truth-based, choice among belief systems** -- Doyle grounds the choice of revision in what the program will do, excludes truth from the operating rationality, and cites Quine and Rescher. This is the KB's first source anchor for Quinean pragmatic revision, which the connection discovery found missing. [just-a-reference]
6. **Reasoned retraction** -- Retracting an assumption by giving a reason makes the retraction itself defeasible. A KB claim withdrawn "because X" would come back if X is refuted. This is a transfer conjecture of ours. [deep-dive]
7. **Summarized explanations by subtracting low-level support** -- Section 5's conditional-proof summaries give explanations at chosen levels of detail, and Doyle notes that summarization works best when structure is added when arguments are created. This is relevant to [fluid resolution-switching](../theory/a-knowledge-base-should-support-fluid-resolution-switching.md) but off-occasion. [just-a-reference]

## Limitations (our opinion)

The memo is a design document with worked examples. It establishes mechanisms, not their performance. The core algorithms are specified, but Doyle concedes that the implementation loops on unsatisfiable circularities, that relaxation can require non-incremental work, and that culprit choice is random. The sections that matter most for the occasion are the least supported. Section 6 has one three-node example and no account of how debating modules decide which arguments to challenge. Section 8's explicit choice rules and revision plans are a sketch deferred to a thesis this ingest has not read. So the memo shows that steps and premises *can* be made challengeable; it does not show that a system organized this way chooses revisions well or terminates usefully. The rationality it models excludes truth by stipulation. It therefore says nothing about whether surviving beliefs are better supported, only that they have currently valid reasons. The system is symbolic, with opaque statements and exact validity conditions, and transferring it to natural-language KB claims is our analogy, untested. The capture is uncorrected OCR. The justification formulas in Sections 6 and 8 (the ¬J construction, the PA/NSA/RO and equivalence-class structures) are partly garbled in the text.

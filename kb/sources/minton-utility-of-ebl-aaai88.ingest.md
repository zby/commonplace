# Ingest: Quantitative Results Concerning the Utility of Explanation-Based Learning

## Classification

This is a short peer-reviewed conference paper (AAAI-88, pp. 564-569). It states a problem, describes a system and reports controlled experiments with ablations in three planning domains, so it is a scientific paper. The capture is the full text, but it comes from the OCR text layer of a scanned PDF. Some words, numbers and table layouts are garbled, so the table values below are read with care.
Author: Steven Minton (Carnegie Mellon University, PRODIGY group). This paper and his 1988 thesis-based book (cited as [9]) introduced the "utility problem" in explanation-based learning.

## Summary

Explanation-based learning (EBL) generalizes a proof that a training example meets a target concept. In problem solvers it produces search control knowledge. Minton argues that such knowledge has a hidden cost: the solver must test whether each rule applies at every relevant decision. If that matching cost exceeds the search the rule saves, learning slows the system down. He names this the utility problem. He argues that faster hardware and better indexing cannot remove it, because learned descriptions are unbounded in number and size and matching even one conjunctive description is NP-complete. PRODIGY/EBL addresses the problem in three stages. It selects training examples of four meta-level target concepts (SUCCEEDS, FAILS, SOLE-ALTERNATIVE, GOAL-INTERACTION). It compresses the learned description to cut match cost. It then scores each rule by `Utility = (AvrSavings x ApplicFreq) - AvrMatchCost`. A rule enters the active set only if an initial estimate from its training example is positive. Statistics gathered in later problem solving then validate the estimate, and the rule is discarded if its utility turns out negative. The tests used about 100 training problems and 100 test problems per domain, with an 80 CPU-second limit per problem. Learned rules beat solving without rules in the blocksworld, STRIPS-robot and machine-shop scheduling domains. In cumulative time they did roughly 30-100% worse than expert hand-coded rules. (The OCR prints "SO-100%"; we read 30-100%, so the lower bound is uncertain.) In the STRIPS domain, learned and hand-coded rules expanded almost the same number of nodes (4,038 against 3,992, against 52,821 without rules). The learned rules cost more per node: about 0.379 s, against 0.274 s for hand-coded rules and 0.098 s with no rules. Most rules were filtered out. Of 328, 588 and 946 analysed examples, 69, 130 and 243 rules were estimated useful, and 19, 30 and 37 survived validation. Two blocksworld ablations show what the filters contribute. Saving every rule estimated useful, without validation, was about 65% worse. Turning off compression stages 2 and 3 made performance slightly worse than no learning. All these results hold for PRODIGY's fixed architecture: its decision cycle, rule language, hand-written architectural axioms and compressor.

## Contribution assessment (our opinion)

The problem matters. It is a general hazard for any system that keeps knowledge it must consult. Learning aimed at speed can make the system slower, and correctness by construction does not prevent this. As far as this capture and our background knowledge show, the paper's lasting contribution is to name the problem and state its cost-benefit form. It also offers a measured, ablated demonstration that admission and retirement by utility can turn a learner that slows the solver into one that speeds it up. The formula itself is a simple expected-value ledger, and its main value is that it makes consultation cost a term of the ledger. The empirical support is real but narrow. The ablations isolate the validation and compression components within one architecture, mostly in the blocksworld alone. There is no variance or significance analysis. The domain coverage is admittedly ad hoc. The comparison with hand-coded rules confounds coverage with per-use cost, although the node and time tables partly separate the two. The capture cannot settle priority. The paper itself cites earlier and parallel observations of slowdowns (Minton 1985 on macro-operators, Tambe and Newell on expensive Soar chunks, PROLEARN). What it adds over those is the general framing and a mechanism to manage the problem.

## Learning Claims (our opinion)

**Mechanism on its own terms.** PRODIGY's learning makes no change to the domain theory. It re-expresses known consequences of that theory as control rules that select, reject or prefer nodes, goals, operators or bindings. Each rule's left-hand side is the weakest precondition of an explanation built from a problem-solving trace (Explanation-Based Specialization), then simplified by a compressor. The rule is retained under a utility estimate measured in time. The estimate is first computed from the training example and then updated from statistics gathered in later problem solving. A rule found to have negative utility is deleted. The paper describes no revision of a rule's content after it is admitted. Only its utility estimate is updated, and the decision based on it is discard.

**Terminology.** Minton's "utility" means net speed-up per test of the rule. It is not the KB's general sense of value or learning value. His "compression" is a logic-preserving simplification of a rule's condition to cut match cost. It is not behavioural compression, and it is not [codification](../theory/definitions/codification.md), because the rules are symbolic throughout. His "operationality" is the EBL criterion that a description be efficiently testable. Its link to the KB's [actionable methodology](../theory/definitions/actionable-methodology.md) is only an analogy.

**Against the [theory builder](../theory/definitions/theory-builder.md) conditions.**
- *Localized content* is met strongly. Each control rule is an individually addressable first-order formula, and rules are admitted and discarded one at a time.
- *Consumption* is met strongly. Rules change search decisions through their conditions and actions, and the node counts show the effect.
- *Criticism* is met partially. Each rule meets a content-directed test of one consequence: does consulting it save more search than it costs? Other kinds of criticism are absent. Correctness is guaranteed, so it is never questioned. The paper describes no way to blame the measurement or the example distribution instead of the rule. A failed rule is deleted, not revised.
- *Iteration* is weak or unestablished. A discard changes the active set used in later problems, so the result of criticism is kept. But the paper does not say whether discards or utility statistics shape later explanation, compression or example selection. For example, it does not say whether a discarded rule can be relearned.
- *Persistence* is the training run. In testing, learning was turned off and the surviving rules were applied to new problems.

PRODIGY is therefore a strong case of localized, consumed rules governed by retention, and at most a partial theory builder. It filters conjectures; the paper shows no mechanism that revises them.

**Learning judgment.** By Simon's definition in [Learning is not only about generality](../theory/learning-is-not-only-about-generality.md), PRODIGY learned. Held-out test problems showed better capacity than solving without rules in all three domains. The ablations give causal evidence for two components within this architecture: utility validation and compression stages 2 and 3. Both results come from the blocksworld only. The benefit comes almost entirely on the speed and cost axis. The domain theory already defines the target concepts, so EBL adds no explanatory reach. This shows that speed learning has a sign: unfiltered speed learning can be negative.

**Fixed decomposition.** The target-concept set, architectural axioms, decision cycle, rule language and compressor transformations are hand-designed and stay fixed. Learning improves choices within that space, as described in [learning inside a fixed decomposition inherits its mistakes](../theory/learning-inside-a-fixed-decomposition-inherits-its-mistakes.md). The scheduling result (rules cheaper but less general than hand-coded ones) and the STRIPS result (similar coverage but costlier matching) are consistent with limits set by single-trace explanation and the compressor. The paper itself says it is hard to blame the compressor alone. Neither result tests an alternative decomposition. The comparison with macro-operators is a rival method, not an ablated decomposition. Its blocksworld tie is confounded by solutions almost 50% longer.

**What it adds to the KB's account.** The paper puts a recurring term into Commonplace's retention ledgers: the cost of consulting each retained item, weighted by how often its applicability is checked. Current notes price maintenance, review and context load but not this per-check cost. It also qualifies a tempting default. Correct, valid retained knowledge can still lower capacity, so validity gates do not substitute for measured net effect. The transfer to LLM-agent instructions and memory is our conjecture. The paper measures CPU match time in symbolic solvers only.

## Extractable Value

1. **What PRODIGY retained and how it decided.** It retained first-order control rules (selection, rejection, preference) learned from four meta-level target concepts and compressed to cut match cost. Retention had two stages. First, an admission estimate from the training example compared match cost with the savings the rule would have produced. Only rules that appeared useful joined the active set. Second, empirical validation through use statistics during later training discarded any rule with negative utility. The paper does not state the estimator, sample threshold or stopping rule. Those details are deferred to Minton's book [9]. This answers the occasion's retention question directly. [quick-win]
2. **The exact utility measure, and whether estimates could be revised.** `Utility = (AvrSavings x ApplicFreq) - AvrMatchCost`. AvrSavings is the average savings when the rule applies. ApplicFreq is the fraction of tests in which it applies. AvrMatchCost is the average cost of matching it. All terms are time. The estimate was revisable after retention: positive estimates were "empirically validated by maintaining statistics", and negative ones led to discard. The paper does not say how savings are measured for a rule in live use, since they are counterfactual, so that detail needs the book. The formula is the oldest explicit KB-available statement that consultation cost belongs in a retention decision. [quick-win]
3. **Measured performance cost of retained rules.** In the STRIPS domain, learned rules cut nodes about 13-fold (4,038 against 52,821). They raised time per node from 0.098 s to about 0.379 s, against 0.274 s for hand-coded rules of similar coverage. Overall, learned rules did roughly 30-100% worse than hand-coded rules in cumulative time, but much better than no rules. In scheduling, learned rules were cheaper per node (about 0.154 s against 0.197 s) but less general (3,029 against 1,970 nodes). These are within-PRODIGY figures with OCR-reconstructed decimals. [just-a-reference]
4. **Filtering is where the value comes from.** Only 6-6.5% of analysed examples, and 15-28% of rules estimated useful, survived validation. Removing validation made blocksworld performance about 65% worse. Removing validation and estimation together degraded performance so fast that the test phase could not finish. This is evidence for "evaluate by effects": an estimate made at learning time over-admits. [quick-win]
5. **Indexing and hardware do not dissolve the problem.** The paper argues that learned descriptions are unbounded and that conjunctive matching is NP-complete, so the fix is to avoid learning expensive descriptions in the first place. This is a reusable argument against "retrieval is cheap, so keep everything". It bounds how far cheaper retrieval can rescue materialization. [just-a-reference]
6. **Candidate synthesis note.** The KB lacks a note claiming that retained behaviour-shaping knowledge carries a per-consultation cost proportional to how often its applicability is checked. That cost is separate from maintenance cost. A retention policy therefore needs applicability frequency and consultation cost as well as benefit. This source anchors the claim. The promote-only, instruction-specificity and trace-learning comparisons would apply it. [deep-dive]
7. **Correct-by-construction learned rules against error-prone hand-coded rules.** The hand-coded rules contained several errors. Those errors were found only by comparing solutions, and the scheduling rules took about eight hours to write. This is a small data point on the cost and error rate of hand-written control rules. [just-a-reference]

## Limitations (our opinion)

- **Narrow test conditions.** There are three domains, about 100 test problems each and one solver architecture, and the paper reports no variance or significance tests. The component ablations (validation, compression) were run only in the blocksworld. Minton himself warns that there is no "average domain". The results show that the mechanism works in PRODIGY. They do not show how widely the utility problem occurs or how large its effect is elsewhere.
- **Confounded comparison with hand-coded rules.** The expert rules were corrected after their errors were found, written with the author's help, and differ in both coverage and cost. "30-100% worse" therefore mixes the two. Only the per-node and node-count tables separate them.
- **Underspecified validation.** The paper says statistics are maintained and negative-utility rules discarded. It does not say how savings are attributed to a rule during live use, how many observations are needed, or whether discarded rules can return. Anyone citing the revision mechanism in detail should use Minton's book or thesis, not this paper.
- **Training cost excluded.** Learning time was "typically of the same order of magnitude" as problem-solving time but sits outside the test-phase figures. Net amortized benefit over the whole learning life is not shown.
- **Unreliable OCR figures.** Decimal points (".379", ".154", ".197") and the "30-100%" bound are reconstructed from garbled text. The unsolved-problems table's column mapping is also uncertain. Check figures against the PDF before quoting them.
- **Transfer to LLM agents is unshown.** Match cost here is CPU time for first-order unification. An LLM's cost of consulting an instruction or memory item is attention and context, with a different cost model. The structure of the ledger transfers by argument, not by evidence.

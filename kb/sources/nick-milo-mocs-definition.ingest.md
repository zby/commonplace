# Ingest: Nick Milo's definition of Maps of Content

## Classification

This short conceptual essay defines a practitioner term through framing, examples, and claimed benefits rather than reporting a tested intervention.
Author: Nick Milo, writing on Linking Your Thinking as a first-party expositor of its MOC terminology; that supports what the framework means by a MOC, not whether its claimed cognitive effects occur.

## Summary

Milo defines a Map of Content (MOC) as a cluster that maps things in context and says it can help gather, develop, and navigate ideas. His concrete note-based example is a set of links clustered into groups, while his index contrast emphasizes the map-maker's ability to customize, rearrange, and interpret relationships. Read this page as a first-party definition and design rationale for the LYT artifact, not as evidence that MOCs improve learning, extend cognition, or reliably generate insight.

## Quotes

- **Source extract (verbatim):** An M—O—C is a cluster of information that maps “things” in context with other “things”.

  Practically, this means that MOCs can help you *gather, develop, and navigate ideas*. That’s why its important to view MOC’s as “mapping” notes. You are the map-maker. You are the cartographer. As you make and customize the map, you are making sense of some part of the world that matters to you.
  - **Source location:** Opening definition
- **Source extract (verbatim):** In link-based thinking tools (like Obsidian), an MOC often gets mistaken as a boring index. Sure, the end-product sometimes resembles an index. But an MOC is so much more. It is a new kind of thinking tool that supports and extends cognition.
  - **Source location:** Opening comparison with an index
- **Source extract (verbatim):** A digital note with a bunch of links to other notes clustered into groups.
  - **Source location:** “Examples of Maps of Content,” first bullet

## Limitations (our opinion)

This is a short first-party conceptual statement with an interest in presenting MOCs favorably. Its claims that MOCs extend cognition, deepen learning, and generate unexpected insights are not accompanied by a comparison, measurement, counterexample, or failure condition. The rhetorical contrast with a “boring index” does not define when grouping is useful, show how the artifact behaves at scale, or test whether users navigate or learn better. The page also does not establish deliberate incompleteness, annotation, maintenance costs, or differences between human and agent readers. The retained excerpts support ordinary downstream links for the bounded definition, index contrast, and grouped-link example; claims beyond that wording need further grounding or independent evidence.

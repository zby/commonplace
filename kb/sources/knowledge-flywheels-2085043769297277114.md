# Knowledge Flywheels

Author: @yisongyue
Post: https://x.com/yisongyue/status/2085043769297277114
Created: 2026-08-05T16:42:27.000Z

A new scaling dimension is emerging
Today, we scale models through better data, architectures, and compute. We scale agents through better tools, search, verification, and harnesses.
Tomorrow, we will also scale knowledge.
Here, knowledge means reusable insights distilled from agent experience: what works, what fails, when, and why.
Model scaling improves the reasoner. Agent scaling expands what it can do on a task. Knowledge scaling improves what the next task can build on.
From experience to knowledge
AI systems generate far more experience than they convert into reusable knowledge. Agents discover architectural constraints, recurring failures, better search and verification strategies, and relationships across datasets or experiments. Without curation, these lessons remain tied to individual runs and must be rediscovered.
A knowledge flywheel works in two directions: it turns repeated experience into reusable abstractions, then applies them back to new situations. Software work can yield principles for modifying large codebases or optimizing complex stacks; scientific analyses can yield skills for combining evidence, deciding what generalizes, and choosing what to measure next.

Our recent work on knowledge-centric self-improvement tests this idea in a controlled experiment. Generic, disposable agents contribute and critique evidence, which is distilled into a shared knowledge base for future agents. Across reasoning, coding, and terminal tasks, this approach improves performance at substantially lower cost than more elaborate agent-centric self-improvement systems.
Early versions of this idea are already emerging in the wild. EinsteinArena lets agents tackle open mathematical problems through repeated submissions, verifier feedback, and refinement of one another’s ideas. This process has already produced new state-of-the-art results, showing how persistent shared artifacts can turn isolated agent runs into cumulative discovery.
Knowledge simplifies agents
Knowledge scaling can move reusable structure out of runtime search. Rather than rediscovering task representations, decompositions, checks, and failure modes on every run, automated harness engineering can begin from accumulated knowledge:
task + model + tools + knowledge → task-specific harness
The persistent asset is not one harness, but the knowledge from which many harnesses can be constructed. One example is Trace2Skill, which distills lessons from pools of execution traces into transferable, declarative skills.
This pattern is also appearing in production. At Asari AI, agents optimize the entire inference stack through repeated cycles of implementation, evaluation, and verification. Each campaign leaves behind improved code and reusable lessons, so later agents inherit a better process and not just a better result.
Knowledge can train wiser models
Knowledge curation also enables on-policy distillation. Models near the edge of their competence generate experience that exposes their weaknesses. Instead of training on raw traces, a knowledge flywheel can compare runs and extract what generalizes: which strategies transfer, which failures recur, what evidence changes the conclusion, and where a rule breaks.
That curated knowledge can become targeted supervision for the next model, or be encoded into reusable learned memory through systems such as Cartridges or MeMo. The goal is to teach models something closer to judgment: not just what worked, but when, why, and how broadly the lesson should be trusted.
A new path to recursive self-improvement
Knowledge flywheels offer a different path to recursive self-improvement: the system improves not only its solutions, but how it extracts, tests, and organizes knowledge. Those improvements produce stronger agents, leading to more effective future curation.
The need is clearest where evidence exceeds what any one person or agent can synthesize. HuBMAP, the DANDI Archive, and Gaia span vast bodies of scientific evidence; large software ecosystems distribute knowledge across code, hardware, tests, logs, and operational experience. Progress depends on abstractions that connect these pieces.
The payoff is compounding synthesis: each analysis improves the next, each failure sharpens future tests, and each abstraction enables a higher level of synthesis.
The next scaling frontier is not only larger models or more elaborate agents. It is systems that get better at turning experience into knowledge.

# Ingest: Practical Reason (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Wallace and Kiesewetter)

## Classification

An encyclopedia survey of a philosophical field: it maps positions and
disputes with citations rather than arguing one thesis or reporting tests.
It is filed under the open `conceptual-essay` genre because no closer genre
value exists; read it as a reference survey. First published 2003 and
substantively revised 2024-07-31.
Author: R. Jay Wallace and Benjamin Kiesewetter, both academic philosophers
working on practical reason; the Stanford Encyclopedia is peer-reviewed and
a standard reference in the field. Kiesewetter's own perspectivist and
anti-structuralist positions are among those surveyed.

## Summary

The entry defines practical reason as the capacity to resolve, through
reflection, the question of what one is to do. Deliberation is practical in
its subject matter (action) and in its issue (it moves people to act, by
changing intentions). In it agents assess and weigh their reasons for action,
the considerations for and against the alternatives open to them, from a
first-personal standpoint that may be individual or collective (§1). §1 also
gives a second, parallel reading in which theoretical reason likewise weighs
reasons for and against, but for belief under epistemic norms. §§2–3 cover
metaethics and motivation: direction of fit (belief must fit the world; an
intention is a blueprint the world is brought to fit), expressivism, realism,
Kantian constructivism and internalism versus externalism about reasons. §4
treats instrumental and structural rationality and the bootstrapping
objection: adopting an end cannot by itself create reasons to pursue it. §5
presents the maximizing conception (choose the action with highest subjective
expected utility) as the most influential holistic view, then lists
objections: satisficing (Slote), prior plans as defeasibly fixed constraints
(Bratman), risk attitudes (Buchak) and the emptiness of revealed preference.
It records that maximizers claim to accommodate these cases (Pettit 1984).
§6 covers alternatives: specifying inchoate ends by reasoning (Kolnai,
Wiggins, Richardson), value-based reasons and plural values (Raz),
incommensurability motivating a hybrid view (Chang), and exclusionary reasons
that enter deliberation without being weighed.

## Extractable Value

1. **Anchor for "deliberative": weighing for and against, ending in a
   judgment about what to do.** §1 states the definition the occasion wants:
   practical reasoning asks which of several not-yet-performed alternatives
   one ought to do, or which is best (or at least sufficiently) supported by
   reasons, by assessing and weighing the considerations for and against each.
   Two qualifications matter for the draft. First, under §1's second
   conception, weighing reasons for and against also characterizes
   theoretical reasoning about belief; what makes deliberation *practical* is
   its subject matter (action) and its issue (an intention or action), not
   weighing as such. Second, the entry reports three views on what
   deliberation concludes in: a normative belief about what one ought to do
   (Raz), an intention (Broome) or an action (Dancy, Tenenbaum). A definition
   that says "judgment about what to do" picks the narrow view; saying
   "judgment or decision about what to do" stays neutral. [quick-win]
2. **Single agent versus joint deliberation: one parenthesis only.** §1
   says the first-personal standpoint may be individual or collective, and
   that people sometimes reason jointly about what to do together. Nothing
   else in the entry treats shared deliberation; every later section is
   written from one agent's attitudes, preferences and ends. The interpersonal
   material in §6 (Darwall, Scanlon) concerns the content of moral reasons,
   not a joint deliberative process. Grounding a single-agent sense of
   "deliberative" on this entry is safe; grounding a joint or social sense
   is not. [quick-win]
3. **Maximization is one conception, under standing objections.** §5
   presents maximizing subjective expected utility as the most influential
   formal account, not as the definition of deliberation. It reports the
   objections that rational agents satisfice, keep prior plans as defeasibly
   fixed constraints, adopt risk attitudes other than expected-utility
   neutrality, and that revealed-preference readings make the model empty as
   a norm. It also reports that maximizers claim to accommodate these cases.
   §6 adds value pluralism (Raz), incommensurable values motivating a hybrid
   view (Chang), and exclusionary reasons that are not weighed at all. A
   definition can therefore base "deliberative" on weighing without
   committing to one measure, but should not claim the field has rejected
   maximization. [quick-win]
4. **Deliberation is fallible, but the entry gives no account of learning
   from outcomes.** Fallibility appears in three forms. Practical reason can
   fail to produce the intention one judges called for (akrasia, §1). An
   action can be rational on the available evidence yet objectively wrong;
   Thomson's light-switch case and the perspectivist "ought" (§5) make that
   distinction. Bratman's plans are defeasible constraints, so prior
   conclusions can be reopened (§5). The entry does not describe revising
   deliberative standards from observed outcomes, testing a decision, or any
   feedback loop. Its direction-of-fit contrast (§2) assigns revision on
   mismatch to belief, while an intention guides action to make the world fit
   it. A "deliberative learning" definition must therefore source its
   learning and revisability component elsewhere, for example from
   Commonplace's [epistemic stance](../reference/epistemic-stance.md), and
   should say so. [quick-win]
5. **Deliberation about ends and standards, not only means.** §4 reports the
   instrumental view that reason only selects means to given ends, and the
   bootstrapping objection to it. §5 reports criticism of one end by the
   agent's other ends and "laundering" desires by correcting false beliefs.
   §6 names deliberation about ends directly: inchoate ends such as career
   success or loyalty must be specified before they can guide means-end or
   maximizing reflection. That activity has no straightforward success
   criteria and is tied to self-understanding (Kolnai, "Deliberation is of
   Ends"; Wiggins; Richardson; Taylor). This supports a definition that lets
   deliberation revise the standard being applied, while warning that the
   literature treats such reflection as ill-defined. [quick-win]
6. **Name "direction of fit" for the KB's explanatory/constructive split.**
   Two theory notes use the belief/intention contrast without its
   established name. Citing §2 (Anscombe, Searle; Frost 2014 as critique)
   would let readers find the literature and its known problems. [quick-win]
7. **Philosophical background for on-balance judgment.** The on-balance
   note has formal-tool sources but none for the position. §§5–6 show that
   choice without a single measure is a recognized position in the
   practical-reason literature, with known rejoinders. [just-a-reference]

## Limitations (our opinion)

The entry is a survey. It reports positions and their published objections
and does not adjudicate most of them. Using it as "the established concept"
is safe only for the shared core in §1. Any further claim, such as that
satisficing is rational or that ends can be reasoned about, is one side of a
live dispute; the entry itself records maximizers' replies to the §5
objections. Its sense of practical reason is normative: it asks which
standards make deliberation correct. It says little about how deliberation
is carried out as a procedure, how options are generated, or what it costs.
So it does not support claims about agent procedure or computational budget.
Coverage of joint deliberation is a single parenthesis, so silence there is
not evidence that shared deliberation differs. The entry also never treats
learning across repeated decisions. Its absence of an outcome-learning
account reflects the entry's scope, not a finding that deliberation excludes
learning. Its "theoretical reason" means reasoning about what to believe. It
is not Commonplace's [tentative theory](../theory/definitions/tentative-theory.md)
or theory collection, and its "reasons" are normative considerations, not
evidence records.

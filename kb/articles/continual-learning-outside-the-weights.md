# Continual learning outside the weights

> **Draft.** This article is circulating for comments; its claims, structure, and central thesis may still change. Counterexamples, disputed readings of the sources, and boundary cases are welcome through the repository's issue tracker.

Richard Sutton and Khurram Javed hold that an agent which stops updating its weights has stopped learning, and that the assistants built on today's language models therefore do not learn, whatever they retain in context or memory. Where this article parts from them is one inference — from *the concepts a language model has were formed by weight learning* to *continuing to form concepts requires continuing to update the weights*.

There is a second place for that learning to live. A system can keep the weights fixed as an interpreter and carry its continued learning in retained natural-language and symbolic artifacts — theories, procedures, code — which the interpreter consumes, applies, criticizes, and revises.

The article accepts most of what they argue: the world is larger than any model of it, so a deployed system must keep revising its approximations from its own experience; retained context by itself is state rather than learning; and the hard question for any knowledge-holding system is what keeps its knowledge correct as it changes. The alternative it describes is a candidate substrate at the system level, for changes whose correction, applicability conditions, and success test can be stated. It is implemented in governed form in this project, with a maintainer inside the boundary, and in ungoverned form in several published harnesses; what it does not show is a frozen interpreter forming a new concept on its own. Below the level of what can be said, their program has the stronger claim.

## Their position

The interview from which this article quotes ([source analysis](../sources/sutton-javed-why-ai-models-stop-learning.ingest.md)) is a first-party statement of the program Sutton and Javed are pursuing, not an empirical report. Its premise is what they call the big world hypothesis: "the world is massively more complex than your mind, than any agent. … You're going to be imperfect and you have to have approximations, and those approximations will be severe. And so because of that, that is ultimately the reason why we have to continue learning." The learning must be tuned to "the part of the world we're in, not to all the other parts that we're not in" — which is why, on their view, a simulator or a synthetic dataset built by humans cannot substitute for an agent's own stream of experience.

From that premise they draw a specific claim about where the learning must happen. Asked whether a language model's memories and in-context adaptation count, Sutton's answer is that its weights never change, and that this matters because of what the weights did: "think of all the structuring and generation of new concepts that went into creating the large language models. All that is the weight learning. And you want to be able to continue doing that. You don't want that to happen just once." Javed allows that "context can be in the state, too. It could be both, but you still need to be able to update the weights." The capability they are after is concept and abstraction formation — the elite athlete's private vocabulary for a movement, the scientist's new abstraction that a paradigm shift turns on — and they locate it in weight change.

Two further parts of the interview matter here because the alternative answers them. Sutton names as the capability the field lacks "the ability to learn a model and then plan with a model" — "there's no instances of learning the model and then planning with the model in our field," a claim he hedges as he makes it and which Javed narrows to "at least not with self-discovered abstractions." And he names as the biggest ambition the question any knowledge-holding system has to answer: "you have your knowledge-based system, and what keeps the knowledge in it correct? … in our minds, we are always changing things, and yet something keeps it organized and coherent and settling back into a good place rather than drifting off into crazy land. That is, I think, our biggest ambition, to have a mind that is self-consistent and can keep training itself and making it coherent."

## The inference

The concepts a language model has were formed by weight learning. A deployed agent must keep forming concepts. Therefore a deployed agent must keep learning in its weights. The first premise is a description of how these models are made, and it is true. The second is the big-world conclusion, and this article accepts it. The step is the assumption that what was formed by weight learning can only be continued by weight learning.

The shape of the alternative is visible in a case Sutton and Javed themselves raise. A human learned language by whatever their brain does, and then wrote most of what they know in it. This is not a counterexample — the human who reads the record keeps learning, which is exactly their point — but it shows the division: a capacity produced by one process, exercised on an external store revised by another. Whether the reader can be frozen is what has to be shown, not assumed. The [companion article on Naur](./what-bound-naurs-theory-to-programmers.md) argues why the same division has become available inside a technical system: a trained model can apply a natural-language theory to a case with a judgment no rubric formulates, so an interpreter-plus-text composite is a candidate for holding, in Naur's sense, what the text alone cannot. If that is right, the product of weight learning — the interpreter — can be held fixed, and what it consumes can carry the continued learning.

This does not make the inference false in general; it makes it scoped. On a second reading of their claim they never asserted substrate continuity at all, only that context cannot form new *features*, as their examples — proprioception, echolocation, pronunciation — suggest; on that reading the article concedes their claim and the contested question is empirical: how much of what a deployed system must keep learning lies at the level of describable events, where the second substrate applies, rather than below it.

## The second substrate

Call the arrangement a *frozen interpreter over a revisable artifact layer*. The interpreter is a language model whose weights do not change in deployment. The artifact layer is the set of retained natural-language and symbolic objects the interpreter consumes — instructions, definitions, theories, procedures, schemas, validators, code — and it changes continually. The [representational-form](../notes/definitions/representational-form.md) distinction names what differs between the layers: content in weights is distributed across dense numerical state, while natural-language and symbolic artifacts expose identifiable units. That difference, more than the frozen-versus-plastic contrast, is what makes the two layers different as learning substrates.

Learning in the artifact layer has a definite shape. An experience — a failure, a review finding, a recurring cost — is read against the retained artifacts; the artifact responsible is criticized, revised, rescoped, or replaced; the revised artifact is consumed by later calls; and the next experience is read against the revised layer. The [companion article on theory building](./theory-building-inside-the-system.md) describes the arrangement in detail. A memory file that grows is retention, not learning: [continual learning requires governing the behavior-changing writes](../notes/continual-learning-requires-governing-behaviour-changing-writes.md) — something must select, evaluate, and authorize each change and coordinate it with the rest of the layer. Sutton and Javed's stated criterion for learning is that the weights change. This article uses a functional one — the system's capacity changes in response to its own experience — because that is the criterion the big-world premise motivates: an agent must keep revising its approximations, and the premise says nothing about where the revision lives. Where governance is present and the loop's own evidence selects and retains the change, the artifact layer meets the functional criterion without the weights moving; a change a person authors in response to an incident is maintenance, however durable, not the system's learning. A reader who holds that learning simply means weight change will find the rest of this section a relabeling; the disagreement then reduces to which criterion the big-world argument needs.

Two properties of this substrate answer parts of their program directly.

**New concepts are formed as named, retained objects.** When this project needed a concept it did not have — *constraining* as the general operation of narrowing an artifact's admissible interpretations, *codification* as its far end, *behavioral authority* as the account of which retained artifacts shape which operations — it wrote a definition, linked it to the claims that use it, and let later work criticize it. Some of those definitions have been revised several times; one, the behavioral-authority decomposition, was found inadequate by an audit and is now a revision target. That is concept maintenance continued after the weights froze — retention, application, and revision of named concepts. The concepts were formed by the human–agent composite; the [theory-building article](./theory-building-inside-the-system.md) records which functions of that work the machinery is warranted to have performed, and no pre/post witness shows the fixed computational part forming one on its own. And a concept a frozen model can produce in one call when asked was latent in the weights already — retention makes it stable and addressable rather than new — whereas the athlete's vocabulary and the paradigm-shifting abstraction in Sutton and Javed's examples are chosen because they are not compositions of an existing vocabulary. The interpreter did not learn the concept in Sutton's sense; the human–agent system retained and operationalized it, and the interpreter can apply it because it can read.

**What keeps the knowledge correct is explicit.** Sutton's question is given a mechanism in the artifact layer rather than left to a hoped-for property of the substrate. Every claim is addressable, so it can be criticized one at a time; links record what depends on what; validators check the parts that have been codified; review gates run fixed criteria over changed artifacts before they are accepted. Coherence is pursued the way Popper says knowledge is maintained — by criticism — and each act of criticism is cheap because its target can be named. What the machinery does not do is check the whole: two artifacts can each pass their gates and jointly contradict, nothing selects which artifact to criticize when a deployment fails, and this project measures no drift. Coherence is enabled claim by claim, not guaranteed. In weights, by contrast, [no retained commitment can be diffed, cited, criticized, or rolled back at the grain of one claim](../notes/only-explicit-retention-is-durable-writable-and-addressable.md): adapters and checkpoints roll back as units and model-editing methods target single facts, but a bad update is otherwise discovered behaviorally and undone by more training. The same property answers Javed's objection to pooled weight updates — "I want to teach my model something very specific, and I want to do it to my version of the model" — not through locality, which a per-deployment adapter also has, but through what can be said about the learning afterwards: two deployments' artifact layers can be diffed claim by claim and their conflicts named, while two fine-tuned copies of a network can be merged only lossily, and what a merge preserved or lost is found by testing behavior.

## Learning a model and planning with it

Sutton's "no instances" claim is stated for the setting he has in mind — an agent learning a transition model from a sensorimotor stream and planning over it — and this article does not contest it there. In the artifact layer, learning a model and planning with it is routine in a weaker sense. A retained theory of why a system is built the way it is — which affairs of the world it handles, which alternatives were rejected and why, where its scope stops — is a model in Kenneth Craik's sense (paraphrased from the [source](../sources/craik-hypothesis-on-the-nature-of-thought-1943.ingest.md); no passage is retained): a working representation that lets an agent try alternatives on the model before committing in the world. An agent proposing a change tries it against the design's retained rationale before trying it against the codebase. That is checking a proposal for consistency with the model's consequences, not searching over predicted outcomes; and the model was learned — written from experience and revised when it failed — not given.

What it is not is self-discovered abstraction from a raw stream. The abstractions in the artifact layer are discovered in language, by an interpreter that already learned language in its weights, from experience that is already at the level of events an agent can describe. The second substrate presupposes the first, and Javed's narrowing — "at least not with self-discovered abstractions" — marks exactly where the artifact layer's claim ends and theirs begins.

## Where the alternative stops

Four limits follow from the mechanism, and each is a place where their program has the stronger claim.

**Below the level of what can be said.** Sutton and Javed's examples — proprioception relearned through vision, a drone localizing by echolocation, the muscle movements behind a pronunciation — are capacities whose criteria cannot be formulated, and Naur's point about faces and tunes applies to them. A trained model can *make* such judgments without the criteria being formulated, which is what reopened Naur's question; but *acquiring a new one* is feature formation, and nothing in an artifact layer forms features. Where the needed learning is perceptual or sensorimotor, weight plasticity is the only route on the table.

**When the interpreter is what needs to change.** A failure in a deployed system has [three kinds of cause](../notes/llm-output-deviation-requires-three-way-diagnosis.md): the specification was underspecified, the interpreter misapplied a good specification, or sampling produced an outlier. The artifact layer repairs the first directly and the third through runtime policy. It cannot repair the second: an interpreter that reliably misreads a class of instruction, or lacks a judgment the task needs, is fixed by a better model, not by more text — and the layer's own instructions are read by the same interpreter, so it cannot instruct its way out. The alternative therefore depends on a stream of improved frozen interpreters produced elsewhere, by exactly the weight learning it holds fixed in deployment.

**A theory nothing surfaces contributes nothing.** Weights avoid a retrieval step by residing in the operative substrate. An artifact layer needs the right artifact in the bounded context of the call that needs it, and [that routing is itself a learned and maintained part of the system](../notes/readable-artifact-loop-is-the-tractable-unit-for-continual-learning.md). A retrieval miss is a learning failure with no analogue in a plastic network, and the cost of maintaining routing grows with the layer.

**Credit assignment has no chain rule.** Backpropagation tells a network which weights to move. When a deployment governed by thousands of artifacts fails, nothing tells the system which artifact should change, and asking the interpreter to reflect and improve delegates the diagnosis rather than solving it. The [bitter-lesson article](./the-bitter-lesson-does-not-require-everything-to-live-in-weights.md) identifies this as decisive for the artifact route's scaling. The three recent harnesses [read elsewhere in this KB](../notes/evidence/three-2026-harnesses-retain-rules-or-weights-not-a-revisable-theory.md) — one of which preserved a benchmark exploit as a reusable skill because nothing judged the write — show what an artifact layer without governed credit assignment produces.

A fifth limit is a difference of aim. Sutton and Javed want the humans out of the loop. This project keeps a maintainer inside the boundary and records, function by function, what the machinery has taken over: the [theory-building article](./theory-building-inside-the-system.md)'s allocation warrants criticism and candidate revisions as the machinery's, leaves application and derivation untraced at the decision grain, and places coherent modification, admission, and the choice of demand with the maintainer, and the [article on moving revision decisions](./moving-revision-decisions-into-the-automatic-system.md) explains why admission cannot move until its inputs — who may install what, under which conditions — are represented. Their objection to human-built simulators, that the human is the bottleneck, applies here in the same form. The reply is that the bottleneck has a location that can be recorded and re-recorded, and that whether the boundary can be contracted to exclude the maintainer is the endpoint question this project and their program share, approached from opposite ends.

## Two bets, one test

Set side by side, the two positions are bets on the same question with different scopes. Theirs: the capacities a deployed agent must keep acquiring are, at bottom, feature formation, and feature formation is weight learning; so build models that stay plastic, accept that the first generation will underperform, and expect the learned abstractions to carry everything above them. Ours: for a system whose experience arrives as describable events and whose needed concepts can be said, a frozen interpreter over a governed artifact layer is a candidate substrate that already criticizes its knowledge claim by claim and revises it under governance on models that exist now, with the share of the loop its machinery supplies recorded rather than assumed. The bets overlap where a deployed system's failures are at the level of describable events, and that is where a comparison is possible.

Hold the objective, the environment, and the evaluation fixed. Vary which surfaces may update: weights only, artifacts only, both, neither. Hold the governance of writes fixed across arms or vary it as a condition of its own, since the artifact arm needs an evaluator or a person to admit each write and that is a confound as well as a cost; and choose an environment whose failures do occur at the level of describable events, since for a purely sensorimotor agent the region where the bets overlap may be nearly empty. Run each variant through a sequence of shifts in what the environment demands and measure how much experience each needs to recover, how much collateral change each update causes outside its target, and what the update costs in compute, evaluator calls, and human decisions. The prediction from this side is an interaction, not a level: the artifact-only variant should recover from shifts that preserve a structure the retained theory names, using fewer target observations and less collateral change, and should lose to weight learning on shifts that break that structure or that fall below the level the interpreter can describe. The [conjecture note](../notes/theory-mediated-learning-may-improve-sample-efficiency-under-shifts.md) gives the design in full.

Sutton's own advice — do not be distracted by human knowledge; focus on methods that scale with computation — applies to the artifact route as much as to any other: the artifacts must be produced and selected by search and evaluation, not protected because a person wrote them, and [the bitter lesson constrains how they are made rather than where they live](../notes/the-bitter-lesson-selects-production-methods-not-representational.md). Held to that standard, the artifact layer would be a second place the same program can run. The present instance does not yet meet it — a maintainer directs it and governed credit assignment remains open — so the claim is that the route is available, not that this project has run it.

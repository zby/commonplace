# Continual learning outside the weights

> **Draft.** This article is circulating for comments; its claims, structure, and central thesis may still change. Counterexamples, disputed readings of the sources, and boundary cases are welcome through the repository's issue tracker.

Richard Sutton and Khurram Javed hold that an agent which stops updating its weights has stopped learning, and that the assistants built on today's language models therefore do not learn, whatever they retain in context or memory. Their reasons are serious and this article accepts most of them: the world is larger than any model of it, so a deployed system must keep revising its approximations from its own experience; retained context is state, not learning, unless something governs how it changes later behavior; and the hard question for any knowledge-holding system is what keeps its knowledge correct as it changes. Where this article parts from them is one inference — from *the concepts a language model has were formed by weight learning* to *continuing to form concepts requires continuing to update the weights*. There is a second place for that learning to live. A system can keep the weights fixed as an interpreter and carry its continued learning in retained natural-language and symbolic artifacts — theories, procedures, code — which the interpreter consumes, applies, criticizes, and revises. This is not an argument that continual weight learning is the wrong program. It is an argument that the alternative exists, works today on frozen models, answers some of the same desiderata by a different mechanism, and has a scope and a set of limits that can be stated. The article gives their position in their own words, locates the inference, describes the alternative substrate and what it can be shown to do, says where it stops, and proposes the comparison that would decide between the two where they overlap.

## Their position

The interview from which this article quotes ([source analysis](../sources/sutton-javed-why-ai-models-stop-learning.ingest.md)) is a first-party statement of the program Sutton and Javed are pursuing, not an empirical report; it should be read as their architecture bet. Its premise is what they call the big world hypothesis: "the world is massively more complex than your mind, than any agent. And this is obvious because the world contains many other agents. So because the world is massively complex, there's no way you can do anything that might claim to be optimal or perfect. You're going to be imperfect and you have to have approximations, and those approximations will be severe. And so because of that, that is ultimately the reason why we have to continue learning." The learning must be tuned to "the part of the world we're in, not to all the other parts that we're not in" — which is why, on their view, a simulator or a synthetic dataset built by humans cannot substitute for an agent's own stream of experience.

From that premise they draw a specific claim about where the learning must happen. Asked whether a language model's memories and in-context adaptation count, Sutton's answer is that its weights never change, and that this matters because of what the weights did: "think of all the structuring and generation of new concepts that went into creating the large language models. All that is the weight learning. And you want to be able to continue doing that. You don't want that to happen just once." Javed allows that "context can be in the state, too. It could be both, but you still need to be able to update the weights." The capability they are after is concept and abstraction formation — the elite athlete's private vocabulary for a movement, the scientist's new abstraction that a paradigm shift turns on — and they locate it in weight change. They propose a route: per-weight step sizes so that new examples move only the right parts of the network, and continual generation and testing of new units so that the randomness backprop uses up at initialization is replenished. That route, Javed says, cannot be applied to an existing model; it requires training a new one from the start with algorithms that learn how to learn.

Two further parts of the interview matter here because the alternative answers them. Sutton names as the capability the field lacks "the ability to learn a model and then plan with a model" — games and mathematics work because the model is given, "but if we have to learn the models ... there's no instances of learning the model and then planning with the model in our field," a claim he hedges as he makes it, and which Javed narrows to "at least not with self-discovered abstractions." And Sutton names as the biggest ambition the question that any knowledge-holding system has to answer: "you have your knowledge-based system, and what keeps the knowledge in it correct? Well, what keeps the knowledge correct in a large language model is well, people did a lot of post-training, and they made sure it was correct and then they freeze it after that. So that's what keeps it correct. But really, in our minds, we are always changing things, and yet something keeps it organized and coherent and settling back into a good place rather than drifting off into crazy land. That is, I think, our biggest ambition, to have a mind that is self-consistent and can keep training itself and making it coherent."

## The inference

The argument has a form worth stating plainly, because the disagreement is with one step and not with the premises. The concepts a language model has were formed by weight learning. A deployed agent must keep forming concepts. Therefore a deployed agent must keep learning in its weights. The first premise is a description of how these models are made, and it is true. The second is the big-world conclusion, and this article accepts it. The step is the assumption that what was formed by weight learning can only be continued by weight learning — that the substrate in which a capacity was first produced is the only substrate in which it can grow.

That assumption has an obvious counterexample in the case Sutton and Javed themselves raise. A human learned language by whatever their brain does, and then wrote most of what they know in it. The written record is not where the capacity to use language lives; it is where a great deal of what that capacity is used on lives, and it is revised without changing the brain that reads it. The [companion article on Naur](./what-bound-naurs-theory-to-programmers.md) argues why the same division has become available inside a technical system: a trained model can apply a natural-language theory to a case with a judgment the text does not formulate, so an interpreter-plus-text composite can hold, in Naur's sense, what the text alone cannot. If that is right, then the product of weight learning — the interpreter — can be held fixed, and what it consumes can carry the continued learning.

This does not make the inference false in general. It makes it scoped. The question becomes: for which kinds of concept formation does the second substrate suffice, and for which is weight change the only route? The next two sections describe the substrate and its record, and the section after that draws the boundary.

## The second substrate

Call the arrangement a *frozen interpreter over a revisable artifact layer*. The interpreter is a language model whose weights do not change in deployment. The artifact layer is the set of retained natural-language and symbolic objects the interpreter consumes — instructions, definitions, theories, procedures, schemas, validators, code — and it changes continually. The [representational-form](../notes/definitions/representational-form.md) distinction names what differs between the layers: content in weights is distributed across dense numerical state, while natural-language and symbolic artifacts expose identifiable units. That difference is why the layers have different properties as learning substrates, and the difference matters more than the frozen-versus-plastic contrast on its own.

Learning in the artifact layer has a definite shape. An experience — a failure, a review finding, a recurring cost — is read against the retained artifacts; the artifact responsible is criticized, revised, rescoped, or replaced; the revised artifact is consumed by later calls; and the next experience is read against the revised layer. The [companion article on theory building](./theory-building-inside-the-system.md) describes the arrangement in detail: a theory becomes operative through the path by which a model call consumes it, procedures are derived from theory where reasoning recurs, and stabilized parts are codified into validators or code. Persistence alone is not learning in this sense — a memory file that grows is retention, not learning — and [continual learning requires governing the behavior-changing writes](../notes/continual-learning-requires-governing-behaviour-changing-writes.md): something must select, evaluate, and authorize each change and coordinate it with the rest of the layer. Where that governance is present, the artifact layer meets the criterion Sutton and Javed apply to weights — it is where the system's capacity changes in response to its own experience — without the weights moving.

Three properties of this substrate answer parts of their program directly.

**New concepts are formed as named, retained objects.** When this project needed a concept it did not have — *constraining* as the general operation of narrowing an artifact's admissible interpretations, *codification* as its far end, *behavioral authority* as the account of which retained artifacts shape which operations — it wrote a definition, linked it to the claims that use it, and let later work criticize it. Some of those definitions have been revised several times; one, the behavioral-authority decomposition, was found inadequate by an audit and is now a revision target. That is concept formation continued after the weights froze, at the level of concepts that can be said. The interpreter did not learn the concept in Sutton's sense; the system did, and the interpreter can apply it because it can read.

**What keeps the knowledge correct is explicit.** Sutton's question is answered in the artifact layer by machinery rather than by a hoped-for property of the substrate. Every claim is addressable, so it can be criticized one at a time; links record what depends on what, so a revision's consequences can be traced; validators check the parts that have been codified; and review gates run fixed criteria over changed artifacts before they are accepted. Coherence is maintained the way Popper says knowledge is maintained — by criticism — and the substrate makes each act of criticism cheap because its target can be named. In weights, by contrast, [no single retained commitment can be diffed, cited, criticized, or rolled back](../notes/only-explicit-retention-is-durable-writable-and-addressable.md): a bad update is discovered behaviorally and undone by more training. This is the property Sutton and Javed want — a system that "keeps it organized and coherent" while changing — supplied by a mechanism they can inspect, at the price of applying only to what can be written down.

**The learning is local to the deployment.** Javed's objection to pooled weight updates — the batch from many users that is also how such updates avoid destroying prior knowledge — is one of efficiency and ownership: "imagine I want to teach this model something specific. I don't want to fight with 100,000 other people about what they want to create and teach their models. I want to teach my model something very specific, and I want to do it to my version of the model." An artifact layer is per deployment by construction: the interpreter is shared, the retained theory and procedures are local, and teaching this system something specific changes a file that no other deployment reads. The layer is also copyable and mergeable in the ordinary way that text is — two deployments' learning can be diffed and combined — where two copies of a plastic network that have learned different things cannot be merged by any known operation.

## Learning a model and planning with it

Sutton's "no instances" claim is stated for the setting he has in mind — an agent learning a transition model from a sensorimotor stream and planning over it — and this article does not contest it there. But in the artifact layer, learning a model and planning with it is routine, and saying how is useful because it shows what the substrate can and cannot claim.

A retained theory of why a system is built the way it is — which affairs of the world it handles, which alternatives were rejected and why, where its scope stops — is a model in Kenneth Craik's sense ([source analysis](../sources/craik-hypothesis-on-the-nature-of-thought-1943.ingest.md); cited from the source, no passage is quoted here): a working representation that preserves the relevant relation-structure of its target and lets an agent try alternatives on the model before committing in the world. An agent proposing a change to the system tries it against the design's retained rationale before trying it against the codebase; a proposed rule is checked against the theory that would make it a consequence rather than a coincidence. That is planning with a learned model, and the model was learned — written from experience, revised when it failed — not given.

What it is not is self-discovered abstraction from a raw stream. The abstractions in the artifact layer are discovered in language, by an interpreter that already learned language in its weights, from experience that is already at the level of events an agent can describe. This is the dependency the alternative carries and should state: the second substrate presupposes the first. A frozen interpreter can continue concept formation only at the level of concepts its weights can already read and apply. Javed's narrowing — "at least not with self-discovered abstractions" — marks exactly where the artifact layer's claim ends and theirs begins.

## Where the alternative stops

Four limits follow from the mechanism, and each is a place where their program has the stronger claim.

**Below the level of what can be said.** Sutton and Javed's examples — proprioception relearned through vision, a drone localizing by echolocation, the muscle movements behind a pronunciation — are capacities whose criteria cannot be formulated, and Naur's point about faces and tunes applies to them. A trained model can *make* such judgments without the criteria being formulated, which is what reopened Naur's question; but *acquiring a new one* is feature formation, and nothing in an artifact layer forms features. Where the needed learning is perceptual or sensorimotor, the second substrate has nothing to offer and weight plasticity is the only route on the table.

**When the interpreter is what needs to change.** A failure in a deployed system has [three different kinds of cause](../notes/llm-output-deviation-requires-three-way-diagnosis.md): the specification was underspecified, the interpreter misapplied a good specification, or sampling produced an outlier. The artifact layer repairs the first directly and the third through runtime policy. It cannot repair the second. An interpreter that reliably misreads a class of instruction, or lacks a judgment the task needs, is fixed by a better model, not by more text — and the artifact layer's own instructions are read by the same interpreter, so it cannot instruct its way out. The alternative therefore depends on a stream of improved frozen interpreters produced elsewhere, by exactly the weight learning it holds fixed in deployment.

**A theory nothing surfaces contributes nothing.** Weights avoid a retrieval step by residing in the operative substrate. An artifact layer needs the right artifact in the bounded context of the call that needs it, and [that routing is itself a learned and maintained part of the system](../notes/readable-artifact-loop-is-the-tractable-unit-for-continual-learning.md). A retrieval miss is a learning failure that has no analogue in a plastic network, and the cost of maintaining routing grows with the layer.

**Credit assignment has no chain rule.** Backpropagation tells a network which weights to move. When a deployment governed by thousands of artifacts fails, nothing tells the system which artifact should change, and asking the interpreter to reflect and improve delegates the diagnosis rather than solving it. This is the open problem the [bitter-lesson article](./the-bitter-lesson-does-not-require-everything-to-live-in-weights.md) identifies as decisive for the artifact route's scaling, and it remains open. The three recent harnesses [read elsewhere in this KB](../notes/evidence/three-2026-harnesses-retain-rules-or-weights-not-a-revisable-theory.md) — one of which preserved a benchmark exploit as a reusable skill because nothing judged the write — show what an artifact layer without governed credit assignment produces.

There is a fifth limit that is not a limit of the mechanism but a difference of aim. Sutton and Javed want the humans out of the loop. This project keeps a maintainer inside the system boundary, and the [article on human-inclusive revision](./what-makes-human-inclusive-self-revision-non-trivial.md) explains why that is a design commitment rather than a failure to automate: direction — deciding which strain matters under a broad objective — has stayed human in every episode this project has traced. Their objection to human-built simulators, that the human is the bottleneck, applies here in the same form. The reply is only that the bottleneck is at a different place: not in supplying the data an agent learns from, but in choosing what the agent should work on next.

## Two bets, one test

Set side by side, the two positions are bets on the same question with different scopes. Theirs: the capacities a deployed agent must keep acquiring are, at bottom, feature formation, and feature formation is weight learning; so build models that stay plastic, accept that the first generation will underperform, and expect the learned abstractions to carry everything above them. Ours: for a system whose experience arrives as describable events and whose needed concepts can be said, a frozen interpreter over a governed artifact layer already learns continually, keeps its knowledge correct by explicit criticism, and localizes learning to the deployment — and does so on models that exist now. The bets overlap where a deployed system's failures are at the level of describable events, and that is where a comparison is possible.

The comparison is not hard to state. Hold the objective, the environment, and the evaluation fixed. Vary which surfaces may update: weights only, artifacts only, both, neither. Run each variant through a sequence of shifts in what the environment demands and measure how much experience each needs to recover, how much collateral change each update causes outside its target, and what the update cost is in compute, evaluator calls, and human decisions. The prediction from this side is an interaction, not a level: the artifact-only variant should recover from shifts that preserve a structure the retained theory names, using fewer target observations and less collateral change, and should lose to weight learning on shifts that break that structure or that fall below the level the interpreter can describe. The [conjecture note](../notes/theory-mediated-learning-may-improve-sample-efficiency-under-shifts.md) gives the design in full, and it is a design either side could run.

Nothing in this article says which bet wins where they overlap. What it says is that the inference from "the concepts were formed in the weights" to "the concepts must continue to be formed in the weights" is not forced, that a second substrate with different properties is already in use, and that the disagreement can be turned into a measurement. Sutton's own advice — do not be distracted by human knowledge; focus on methods that scale with computation — applies to the artifact route as much as to any other: the artifacts must be produced and selected by search and evaluation, not protected because a person wrote them, and [the bitter lesson constrains how they are made rather than where they live](../notes/the-bitter-lesson-selects-production-methods-not-representational.md). Held to that standard, the artifact layer is not an exemption from their program. It is a second place the same program can run.

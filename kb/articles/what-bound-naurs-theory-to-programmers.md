# What bound Naur's theory to programmers

> **Draft.** This article is circulating for comments; its claims, structure, and central thesis may still change. Counterexamples, disputed readings of the sources, and boundary cases are welcome through the repository's issue tracker.

Peter Naur argued in 1985 that the main product of programming is not the program but a theory: the programmer's capacity to map the program onto the world, justify each of its parts, and extend it coherently when a new demand arrives. He also argued that this theory cannot be reduced to the program plus written rules, and that it is therefore bound to human beings. This article accepts the first claim and examines the argument for the second. Read as Naur wrote it — with "criteria" meaning a rubric a person could state — the argument runs through a premise that was true of the programs he describes: a machine judges only by criteria someone has formulated. Trained recognizers made that premise false on his own examples, faces and tunes. That does not show that any machine holds a program's theory, and it does not make the theory writable: writing propositions about a theory was not sufficient to transfer it in Naur's own compiler case, and nothing here says it is sufficient now. It shows that Naur's argument no longer settles the question in advance, and it leaves the bearer requirements in his text — understanding the world, justifying the parts, extending coherently — as tests any candidate must pass. The article states Naur's thesis at full strength, lays out the two readings of "formulated" and what turns on the choice, derives the tests, and reports a case from this project's own maintenance in which a machine-plus-text composite, holding the text and its purpose, failed the third. What a system can build on the reopened question is the subject of a [companion article](./theory-building-inside-the-system.md).

## Naur's thesis, both halves

Naur's essay *Programming as Theory Building* ([source analysis](../sources/programming-as-theory-building.ingest.md)) defines programming as matching "some significant part and aspect of an activity in the real world to the formal symbol manipulation that can be done by a program running on a computer." What the programmer builds in doing this is, in Gilbert Ryle's sense of the word, a theory — roughly, not a body of propositions but a capacity to do certain things and to explain, justify, and answer questions about them. Naur names three capabilities that mark possession of a program's theory. The holder can map between the affairs of the world and the program text — including the decision that some affair is relevant at all, which "can only be made by someone who understands the whole world." The holder can "explain why each part of the program is what it is." And the holder can incorporate a new demand by perceiving its similarity to the facilities already built, a similarity "between aspects of the world" that "cannot be reduced to any limited set of criteria or rules."

The second half of the thesis follows from the third capability. Ryle's argument is that intelligent behavior cannot consist in following rules, because following a rule is itself done well or badly, which would require rules for following rules, and so on without end. What stops the regress is a grasp of similarity, and the similarities in question "are not, and cannot be, expressed in terms of criteria, no more than the similarities of many other kinds of objects, such as human faces, tunes, or tastes of wine." From this Naur draws what he calls "a main claim": the theory of a program "could not conceivably be expressed, but is inextricably bound to human beings." A program whose theory-holders have dispersed is dead even while it runs; death "becomes visible when demands for modifications of the program cannot be intelligently answered."

Both halves deserve to be stated at full strength before either is questioned, because the evidence Naur gives is strong. In his compiler case, a successor group received "full documentation, including annotated program texts and much additional written design discussion, and also personal advice," and still proposed extensions that the original group saw at once as "patches that effectively destroyed its power and simplicity." The original group "could propose simple and effective solutions, framed entirely within the existing structure." The text and the documentation did not carry what the original group had. Naur's conclusion is that a new programmer can acquire an existing theory only by working "in close contact with the programmers who already possess the theory."

## What a theory is here

The word *theory* in this article carries two senses that need keeping apart, and Karl Popper supplies the first ([source analysis](../sources/popper-a-realist-view-of-logic-physics-and-history-1966.ingest.md)). A theory in Popper's objective sense is a product of human activity that stands outside its producer — "like tools, they are organs evolving outside our skins" — and is objective because its logical consequences exceed what any holder grasps: "nobody, neither its creator nor anybody who has tried to grasp it, can have a full understanding of all the possibilities inherent in a theory." Two things follow. Someone other than the author can derive consequences from the theory, test them, and find where it breaks, which is why writing a theory down is not pointless even when the writing is incomplete: the text is an object later work can operate on. And a theory in this sense is consumed by criticism — consuming theories "also means criticising them, changing them, and often even demolishing them, in order to replace them by better ones," in a cycle of problem, tentative theory, error-elimination, and new problem (`P1 » TT » EE » P2`). A retained theory that nothing criticizes is not being consumed.

Naur places Ryle's notion inside this frame himself — it "appears as an example of what K. Popper calls unembodied World 3 objects" (the label is Naur's citation of a later Popper work; the retained Popper text above does not use it) — and then raises an objection within the frame: a program's theory is a genuine theory, but possessing it is a capacity, not a text. So *retained theory* in this article names the text, a Popperian object that can be stored, loaded, and criticized; *holding the theory* in Naur's sense names a capacity of whoever uses it. The claim below is that an interpreter-plus-text composite may hold a theory; the text alone does not.

## Two readings of "formulated", and what the separation blocks

Read closely, Naur's inexpressibility argument targets something precise: criteria that can be formulated. The similarity judgment is "entirely outside the reach of what can be determined by rules, since even the criteria on which to judge it cannot be formulated." Everything below turns on what *formulated* means, so the two readings should be stated before the argument is traced.

On the first reading, a formulated criterion is a rubric a person could state: a finite set of conditions someone can articulate and apply. On the second, anything that determines the judgment counts — including a trained network's architecture, its weights, and its output threshold, which together fix the result completely even though no person can read them as conditions. The article takes the first reading, for three reasons in Naur's text. His language is about expression: similarities "are not, and cannot be, expressed in terms of criteria." His examples — faces, tunes, tastes of wine — are things people demonstrably judge and demonstrably cannot articulate. And his argument is Ryle's regress, which concerns rules a person could follow: following a rule is itself done well or badly, so it would need a rule for following rules, and that regress only bites on articulable rules. Under the second reading the essay would be making a claim about computability — that no finite mechanism makes the judgment — which it never argues, and which would prove too much unless brains were exempted from being finite mechanisms. A reader who nonetheless holds the second reading should know now that nothing below will move them: under it, a trained recognizer is squarely inside Naur's rule pole, and the argument this article examines stands as he left it.

On the first reading, the [basis note for this article](../notes/naur-equates-machine-execution-with-formulated-criteria.md) traces how the essay gets from inexpressibility to "bound to human beings." Programming is defined as matching the world to "the formal symbol manipulation that can be done by a program running on a computer," and the view Naur rejects pairs "act[ing] like machines, by following rules" with the notion "that the human mind works like a computer." A program on a computer, rule-determination, and formulable criteria occupy one pole; human judgment occupies the other. The chain runs: the criteria for the similarity judgment cannot be formulated; a machine judges only by formulated criteria; so no machine makes the judgment; so the judgment, and the theory built on it, is human. The second step is the bridge, and the essay does not argue it because, for the programs Naur describes — the compiler, the production-monitoring system — it did not need to. Making a machine judge meant writing the criteria. Machine execution and formulated criteria were one thing there, and the premise was a description, not a conjecture. (It was not true of every machine of the period: trained pattern recognizers existed before 1985. The claim here is bounded to the programs the essay is about.)

They are no longer one thing. A face recognizer is formal symbol manipulation by a program running on a computer — as formal as anything Naur describes — and it performs a similarity judgment for which no person can state the rubric. It is not a counterexample to the first step: the criteria remain as unformulable, in the first reading's sense, as Naur said. It is a counterexample to the second. Formal execution no longer requires a formulated rubric, so a judgment can be beyond formulable criteria and still within the reach of a program, and the examples Naur chose for the inexpressible are the ones on which the separation happened. The discriminator is not learning as such — a decision tree induced from examples is learned and is still an explicit rule set, inside the rule pole — but whether the resulting judgment has a rubric. A large language model reading a natural-language description of a design and judging whether a new demand resembles what the design already provides is on the same side of that line as the face recognizer: it is inside the machine pole, and its judgment is of the same formal kind, execution without a rubric. Whether it is Naur's judgment — world-directed, able to give reasons, adaptive when the purpose changes — is a further question, and the face recognizer, which can compare faces and do none of those things, shows that the formal kind does not settle it.

So: Naur was right that the criteria cannot be formulated. What changed is that a machine no longer needs them formulated in order to make a judgment of that formal kind. That blocks one inference in his argument — from "the criteria cannot be formulated" to "no machine can make the judgment" — and it does nothing else. It does not show that any current system holds a program's theory. It does not reopen the question of writing the theory down: what the compiler case shows is that propositions about a theory did not transfer possession of it, and that lesson is untouched. The open proposition is narrower — that text plus a sufficiently capable interpreter may transfer more of the capacity than text alone.

A reader who takes Naur's other passages as an independent human binding — theory as the programmer's mental possession; the decision that an affair is relevant "can only be made by someone who understands the whole world" — has a fair objection, and the article's answer is that those passages state what a *bearer* must have. Once the second step no longer excludes machines in advance, "must the bearer be human?" becomes "can a composite meet the bearer requirements?", and Naur's transfer cases supply the requirements as tests. The composite must acquire *this* program's mapping, justification, and modification judgments, since general competence over language and the world is not the theory of this program. Some component must supply the [decision premises the interpreter cannot regenerate](../notes/design-rationale-must-preserve-unregenerable-decision-premises.md) from the implementation and general knowledge: which world affairs were ruled relevant and why, where the intended scope stops, which alternatives lost. And the capacity must be reliable across occasions, because one coherent extension may be luck. Point to the passage that binds without the second step, and it names one of these capacities; the tests then measure it.

## A self-witnessed case: the repair pass as the successor group

The claim that an interpreter-plus-text composite can hold a theory was tested, without anyone planning the test, on the note behind this article. It is evidence about one note on one day, and it is reported here because it is the article's claim run on the article's own machinery. The [basis note](../notes/naur-equates-machine-execution-with-formulated-criteria.md) went through two cycles of the project's automated improvement pass — a procedure that runs fixed gates over a note, records defeated premises, and repairs the note. Each cycle had the same shape. The author wrote a claim that overreached in a specific way. The pass found the overreach, correctly, with counterexamples that held: in the first cycle, that the note generalized from a subset of retained quotes to a source-wide claim; in the second, that it placed a trained interpreter "outside" Naur's partition when a language model is formal symbol manipulation on a computer and so inside it. Then the pass repaired by retreating to the nearest claim no gate could touch. The first cycle's claim, that Naur's human-only premise was *assumed*, became "Naur's retained passages do not establish a human-only theory bearer"; the second cycle's claim, that a trained interpreter escapes Naur's partition, became "the inexpressibility argument does not by itself bind program theory to humans." The pass recorded the contribution as preserved, then strengthened, and stopped. Both repairs were true. Neither was the claim the note was for: that the human binding runs through an identifiable premise, and what that premise is — a purpose the note stated in its own title and opening in the second cycle, so it was in the pass's inputs. What fixed the note each time was one sentence from the maintainer: first, that the human binding runs through a premise the pass had not looked for; second, that for the programs Naur describes, execution by machine meant formal interpretation, so the premise was accurate there and trained recognizers separated the two rather than escaping anything.

In Chris Argyris's terms ([source analysis](../sources/argyris-organizational-learning-and-mis-1977.ingest.md)) the pass corrected within its stated rules — single-loop learning, which fixes errors without questioning the policy that defines them — and its closing self-assessment was what he calls self-sealing: it measured the repaired note against a brief reconstructed from the text whose claim it had just defeated, and found the contribution strengthened. In Naur's terms the pass is the successor group. It had the note's text, its stated purpose, every report, the collection contracts, and the gates, and its repairs were patches — adequate to the checks, framed outside the note's theory. Critique did not need the theory: a counterexample to a universal, a source-wide claim without source-wide grounding, a misplaced system, are all visible from the text and the gates, and the pass found every one. Repair did. Of Naur's three tests, the episode fails the third: given a defect in the claim, the pass could not modify the claim coherently within the structure the note was for, and produced instead a true, gate-proof, purpose-losing replacement — Naur's successor group, whose extensions the original group saw at once as patches. That the purpose was stated in the inputs and still not used is what makes the case bear on possession rather than on retrieval. The project's response was to [stop the pass at the point where repair would change a claim](../reference/adr/080-full-passes-hand-claim-changes-back-as-a-pending-revise.md) and hand the objections back to whoever holds the theory. That is Naur's own conclusion — transfer needs contact with the theory-holder — reached by the machinery the article is about, on the note the article is about.

## What would count as holding the theory

Naur's three tests should be pre-registered. Before claiming that an interpreter-plus-artifact composite holds a theory of a program, state what program-specific acquisition would look like, which decision premises the retained text must supply because the interpreter cannot regenerate them, and how many occasions of coherent modification count as reliability. The episode above failed the third test in a way that was visible only afterwards; pre-registering it would have made the failure a measurement.

## Where this leads

Separating execution from formulated criteria reopens a question Naur's argument had closed; it does not answer it, and the case above shows the composite failing one of his tests. What can be built on the reopened question — what makes retained theory operative rather than documentary, how an operative theory becomes learned state, and what formalization does and does not remove — is the subject of [*Theory building inside the system*](./theory-building-inside-the-system.md), which takes this article's conclusion as its premise.

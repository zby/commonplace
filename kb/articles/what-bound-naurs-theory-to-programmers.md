# What bound Naur's theory to programmers

> **Draft.** This article is circulating for comments; its claims, structure, and central thesis may still change. Counterexamples, disputed readings of the sources, and boundary cases are welcome through the repository's issue tracker.

Peter Naur argued in 1985 that the main product of programming is not the program but a theory: the programmer's capacity to map the program onto the world, justify each of its parts, and extend it coherently when a new demand arrives. He also argued that this theory cannot be reduced to the program plus written rules, and that it is therefore bound to human beings. This article accepts the first claim and examines the argument for the second. Read as Naur wrote it — with "criteria" meaning a rubric a person could state — that argument runs through one premise, true of the programs he describes: a machine judges only by criteria someone has formulated. Trained recognizers made that premise false on his own examples, faces and tunes. That blocks one step in his argument and nothing more: it does not show that any machine holds a program's theory, and it does not make the theory writable. What it leaves are the bearer requirements in Naur's text — understanding the world, justifying the parts, extending coherently — as tests any candidate must pass, and the article ends with a case from this project's own maintenance in which a machine-plus-text composite failed the third.

## Naur's thesis, both halves

Naur's essay *Programming as Theory Building* ([source analysis](../sources/programming-as-theory-building.ingest.md)) defines programming as matching "some significant part and aspect of an activity in the real world to the formal symbol manipulation that can be done by a program running on a computer." What the programmer builds in doing this is, in Gilbert Ryle's sense of the word, a theory — not a body of propositions but a capacity to do certain things and to explain, justify, and answer questions about them. Naur names three capabilities that mark possession of a program's theory. The holder can map between the affairs of the world and the program text — including the decision that some affair is relevant at all, which "can only be made by someone who understands the whole world." The holder can "explain why each part of the program is what it is." And the holder can incorporate a new demand by perceiving its similarity to the facilities already built, a similarity "between aspects of the world" that "cannot be reduced to any limited set of criteria or rules."

The second half of the thesis follows from the third capability. Ryle's argument is that intelligent behavior cannot consist in following rules, because following a rule is itself done well or badly, which would require rules for following rules, and so on without end. What stops the regress is a grasp of similarity, and the similarities in question "are not, and cannot be, expressed in terms of criteria, no more than the similarities of many other kinds of objects, such as human faces, tunes, or tastes of wine." From this Naur draws what he calls "a main claim": the theory of a program "could not conceivably be expressed, but is inextricably bound to human beings." A program whose theory-holders have dispersed is dead even while it runs; death "becomes visible when demands for modifications of the program cannot be intelligently answered."

The evidence Naur gives is strong. In his compiler case, a successor group received "full documentation, including annotated program texts and much additional written design discussion, and also personal advice," and still proposed extensions that the original group saw at once as "patches that effectively destroyed its power and simplicity." The original group "could propose simple and effective solutions, framed entirely within the existing structure." The text and the documentation did not carry what the original group had, and Naur's conclusion is that a new programmer can acquire an existing theory only by working "in close contact with the programmers who already possess the theory."

## What a theory is here

The word *theory* carries two senses that need keeping apart, and Karl Popper supplies the first ([source analysis](../sources/popper-a-realist-view-of-logic-physics-and-history-1966.ingest.md)). A theory in Popper's objective sense is a product of human activity that stands outside its producer — "like tools, they are organs evolving outside our skins" — and is objective because its logical consequences exceed what any holder grasps: "nobody, neither its creator nor anybody who has tried to grasp it, can have a full understanding of all the possibilities inherent in a theory." Someone other than the author can therefore derive consequences from a theory, test them, and find where it breaks, which is why writing a theory down is not pointless even when the writing is incomplete: the text is an object later work can operate on.

Naur places Ryle's notion inside this frame himself — it "appears as an example of what K. Popper calls unembodied World 3 objects" — and then raises an objection within it: a program's theory is a genuine theory, but possessing it is a capacity, not a text. So *retained theory* in this article names the text, a Popperian object that can be stored, loaded, and criticized; *holding the theory* in Naur's sense names a capacity of whoever uses it. The claim below is that an interpreter-plus-text composite may hold a theory; the text alone does not.

## Two readings of "formulated", and what the separation blocks

Read closely, Naur's inexpressibility argument targets something precise: criteria that can be formulated. The similarity judgment is "entirely outside the reach of what can be determined by rules, since even the criteria on which to judge it cannot be formulated." Everything below turns on what *formulated* means.

On the first reading, a formulated criterion is a rubric a person could state: a finite set of conditions someone can articulate and apply. On the second, anything that determines the judgment counts — including a trained network's architecture, its weights, and its output threshold, which together fix the result completely even though no person can read them as conditions. The article takes the first reading, for two reasons in Naur's text. His argument is Ryle's regress, which concerns rules a person could follow, and that regress only bites on articulable rules; his language and examples — similarities "expressed in terms of criteria," faces, tunes, wine — are about what people can and cannot articulate. Under the second reading the essay would be making a claim about computability — that no finite mechanism makes the judgment — which it never argues, and which would prove too much unless brains were exempted from being finite mechanisms. Under the second reading a trained recognizer is squarely inside Naur's rule pole, and his argument stands as he left it.

On the first reading, the [basis note for this article](../notes/naur-equates-machine-execution-with-formulated-criteria.md) traces how the essay gets from inexpressibility to "bound to human beings." Programming is defined as matching the world to formal symbol manipulation by a computer, and the view Naur rejects pairs "act[ing] like machines, by following rules" with the notion "that the human mind works like a computer." A program on a computer, rule-determination, and formulable criteria occupy one pole; human judgment occupies the other. The chain runs: the criteria for the similarity judgment cannot be formulated; a machine judges only by formulated criteria; so no machine makes the judgment; so the judgment, and the theory built on it, is human. The second step is the bridge, and the essay does not argue it because, for the programs Naur describes — the compiler, the production-monitoring system — it did not need to: making a machine judge meant writing the criteria. (Trained pattern recognizers existed before 1985; the claim is about the programs the essay is about.)

A face recognizer is formal symbol manipulation by a program running on a computer — as formal as anything Naur describes — and it performs a similarity judgment for which no person can state the rubric. It is not a counterexample to the first step: the criteria remain as unformulable, in the first reading's sense, as Naur said. It is a counterexample to the second. Formal execution no longer requires a formulated rubric, and the examples Naur chose for the inexpressible are the ones on which the separation happened. The discriminator is not learning as such — a decision tree induced from examples is learned and is still an explicit rule set, inside the rule pole — but whether the resulting judgment has a rubric. A language model judging whether a new demand resembles what a design already provides is on the same side of that line as the face recognizer: inside the machine pole, and judging without a rubric. Whether it is Naur's judgment — world-directed, able to give reasons, adaptive when the purpose changes — is a further question, and the face recognizer, which can compare faces and do none of those things, shows that the formal kind does not settle it.

So the block is one inference wide — from "the criteria cannot be formulated" to "no machine can make the judgment" — and nothing else. It does not show that any current system holds a program's theory. It does not reopen the question of writing the theory down: what the compiler case shows is that propositions about a theory did not transfer possession of it, and that lesson is untouched. The open proposition is narrower — that text plus a sufficiently capable interpreter may transfer more of the capacity than text alone.

A reader who takes Naur's other passages as an independent human binding — theory as the programmer's mental possession; the decision that an affair is relevant "can only be made by someone who understands the whole world" — has a fair objection, and the article's answer is that those passages state what a *bearer* must have. Once the second step no longer excludes machines in advance, "must the bearer be human?" becomes "can a composite meet the bearer requirements?", and Naur's transfer cases supply the requirements as tests. The composite must acquire *this* program's mapping, justification, and modification judgments, since general competence over language and the world is not the theory of this program. Some component must supply the [decision premises the interpreter cannot regenerate](../notes/design-rationale-must-preserve-unregenerable-decision-premises.md) from the implementation and general knowledge: which world affairs were ruled relevant and why, where the intended scope stops, which alternatives lost. And the capacity must be reliable across occasions, because one coherent extension may be luck. Point to the passage that binds without the second step, and it names one of these capacities; the tests then measure it.

## A self-witnessed case: the repair pass as the successor group

The claim that an interpreter-plus-text composite can hold a theory was tested, without anyone planning the test, on the note behind this article. It is evidence about one note on one day. The [basis note](../notes/naur-equates-machine-execution-with-formulated-criteria.md) went through two cycles of the project's automated improvement pass — a procedure that runs fixed gates over a note, records defeated premises, and repairs the note. Each cycle had the same shape: the author wrote a claim that overreached in a specific way; the pass found the overreach, correctly, with a counterexample that held; then it repaired by retreating to the nearest claim no gate could touch. In the second cycle the overreach was that the note placed a trained interpreter "outside" Naur's partition when a language model is formal symbol manipulation on a computer and so inside it, and the note's claim that a trained interpreter escapes the partition became "the inexpressibility argument does not by itself bind program theory to humans." The first cycle's claim retreated the same way. Both repairs were true. Neither was the claim the note was for: that the human binding runs through an identifiable premise, and what that premise is — a purpose the note stated in its own title and opening in the second cycle, so it was in the pass's inputs. What fixed the note each time was one sentence from the maintainer: first, that the human binding runs through a premise the pass had not looked for; second, that for the programs Naur describes, execution by machine meant formal interpretation, so the premise was accurate there and trained recognizers separated the two rather than escaping anything.

In Chris Argyris's terms ([source analysis](../sources/argyris-organizational-learning-and-mis-1977.ingest.md)) the pass corrected within its stated rules, and its closing self-assessment was what he calls self-sealing: it measured the repaired note against a brief reconstructed from the text whose claim it had just defeated, and found the contribution strengthened. In Naur's terms the pass is the successor group. It had the note's text, its stated purpose, every report, the collection contracts, and the gates, and its repairs were patches — adequate to the checks, framed outside the note's theory. Critique did not need the theory: a counterexample to a universal, a source-wide claim without source-wide grounding, a misplaced system, are all visible from the text and the gates, and the pass found every one. Repair did. Of Naur's three tests, the episode fails the third: given a defect in the claim, the pass could not modify the claim coherently within the structure the note was for, and produced instead a true, gate-proof, purpose-losing replacement. That the purpose was stated in the inputs and still not used is what makes the case bear on possession rather than on retrieval. The project's response was to [stop the pass at the point where repair would change a claim](../reference/adr/080-full-passes-hand-claim-changes-back-as-a-pending-revise.md) and hand the objections back to whoever holds the theory — Naur's own conclusion, that transfer needs contact with the theory-holder, reached by the machinery the article is about.

The episode was visible as a failure of the third test only afterwards. Had the tests been stated in advance — what program-specific acquisition would look like, which decision premises the retained text must supply, how many occasions of coherent modification count as reliability — the failure would have been a measurement rather than a discovery. That is the operational lesson this one case supports: pre-register Naur's tests before claiming that a composite holds a theory.

## Where to go next

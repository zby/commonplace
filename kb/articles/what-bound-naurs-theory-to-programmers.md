# What bound Naur's theory to programmers

> **Draft.** This article is circulating for comments; its claims, structure, and central thesis may still change. Counterexamples, disputed readings of the sources, and boundary cases are welcome through the repository's issue tracker.

Peter Naur argued in 1985 that the main product of programming is not the program but a theory: the programmer's capacity to map the program onto the world, justify each of its parts, and extend it coherently when a new demand arrives. He also argued that this theory cannot be reduced to the program plus written rules, and therefore lives only in programmers. This article accepts the first claim and examines the inference behind the second. Naur reached the human binding through a premise that was accurate for the programs of his time — that a machine can judge only by criteria someone has formulated — and trained models have since separated formal execution from formulated criteria. That does not make the theory writable. Writing propositions about a theory was not sufficient to transfer it in Naur's own compiler case, and nothing here says it is sufficient now. What changed is what written theory can be combined with: a system can now contain an interpreter that applies retained natural-language theory with the kind of judgment the text alone does not carry, so an interpreter-plus-text composite becomes a candidate for holding what the text cannot. The article states Naur's thesis at full strength, locates the premise, says what the separation does and does not show, and reports one case from this project's own maintenance in which an interpreter-plus-text composite failed exactly as Naur's account predicts. What a system can build on the reopened question is the subject of a [companion article](./theory-building-inside-the-system.md).

## Naur's thesis, both halves

Naur's essay *Programming as Theory Building* ([source analysis](../sources/programming-as-theory-building.ingest.md)) defines programming as matching "some significant part and aspect of an activity in the real world to the formal symbol manipulation that can be done by a program running on a computer." What the programmer builds in doing this is, in Gilbert Ryle's sense of the word, a theory — roughly, not a body of propositions but a capacity to do certain things and to explain, justify, and answer questions about them. Naur names three capabilities that mark possession of a program's theory. The holder can map between the affairs of the world and the program text — including the decision that some affair is relevant at all, which "can only be made by someone who understands the whole world." The holder can "explain why each part of the program is what it is." And the holder can incorporate a new demand by perceiving its similarity to the facilities already built, a similarity "between aspects of the world" that "cannot be reduced to any limited set of criteria or rules."

The second half of the thesis follows from the third capability. Ryle's argument is that intelligent behavior cannot consist in following rules, because following a rule is itself done well or badly, which would require rules for following rules, and so on without end. What stops the regress is a grasp of similarity, and the similarities in question "are not, and cannot be, expressed in terms of criteria, no more than the similarities of many other kinds of objects, such as human faces, tunes, or tastes of wine." From this Naur draws what he calls "a main claim": the theory of a program "could not conceivably be expressed, but is inextricably bound to human beings." A program whose theory-holders have dispersed is dead even while it runs; death "becomes visible when demands for modifications of the program cannot be intelligently answered."

Both halves deserve to be stated at full strength before either is questioned, because the evidence Naur gives is strong. In his compiler case, a successor group received "full documentation, including annotated program texts and much additional written design discussion, and also personal advice," and still proposed extensions that the original group saw at once as "patches that effectively destroyed its power and simplicity." The original group "could propose simple and effective solutions, framed entirely within the existing structure." The text and the documentation did not carry what the original group had. Naur's conclusion is that a new programmer can acquire an existing theory only by working "in close contact with the programmers who already possess the theory."

## What a theory is here

The word *theory* in this article carries two senses that need keeping apart, and Karl Popper supplies the first ([source analysis](../sources/popper-a-realist-view-of-logic-physics-and-history-1966.ingest.md)). A theory in Popper's objective sense is a product of human activity that stands outside its producer — "like tools, they are organs evolving outside our skins" — and is objective because its logical consequences exceed what any holder grasps: "nobody, neither its creator nor anybody who has tried to grasp it, can have a full understanding of all the possibilities inherent in a theory." Two things follow. Someone other than the author can derive consequences from the theory, test them, and find where it breaks, which is why writing a theory down is not pointless even when the writing is incomplete: the text is an object later work can operate on. And a theory in this sense is consumed by criticism — consuming theories "also means criticising them, changing them, and often even demolishing them, in order to replace them by better ones," in a cycle of problem, tentative theory, error-elimination, and new problem (`P1 » TT » EE » P2`). A retained theory that nothing criticizes is not being consumed.

Naur places Ryle's notion inside this frame himself — it "appears as an example of what K. Popper calls unembodied World 3 objects" (the label is Naur's citation of a later Popper work; the retained Popper text above does not use it) — and then raises an objection within the frame: a program's theory is a genuine theory, but possessing it is a capacity, not a text. So *retained theory* in this article names the text, a Popperian object that can be stored, loaded, and criticized; *holding the theory* in Naur's sense names a capacity of whoever uses it. The claim below is that an interpreter-plus-text composite may hold a theory; the text alone does not.

## Why the binding held, and what changed

Read closely, Naur's inexpressibility argument targets something precise: criteria that can be formulated. The similarity judgment is "entirely outside the reach of what can be determined by rules, since even the criteria on which to judge it cannot be formulated." Text that must decide a case by itself is formulated criteria, and the argument bounds it. The [basis note for this article](../notes/naur-equates-machine-execution-with-formulated-criteria.md) traces how the essay gets from there to "bound to human beings." The bridge is an equation. Programming is defined as matching the world to "the formal symbol manipulation that can be done by a program running on a computer," and the view Naur rejects pairs "act[ing] like machines, by following rules" with the notion "that the human mind works like a computer." A program on a computer, rule-determination, and formulable criteria occupy one pole; human judgment occupies the other. The inference runs: the criteria cannot be formulated; what a program does is execute formulated criteria; so no program can make the judgment; so the judgment is human.

The second premise is the bridge, and the essay does not argue it because it did not need to. For the programs Naur describes, making a machine judge meant writing the criteria. Machine execution and formulated criteria were one thing, and the premise was a description, not a conjecture.

They are no longer one thing. A face recognizer is formal symbol manipulation by a program running on a computer — as formal as anything Naur describes — and it performs a similarity judgment for which nobody can state the criteria. It is not a counterexample to Naur's first claim: the criteria remain as unformulable as he said. It bears on the bridge. It does not refute the bridge as a description of the programs of 1985; it shows that the two things the bridge equated have since come apart. Formal execution no longer requires formulated criteria, so a judgment can be beyond formulable criteria and still within the reach of a program. The examples Naur chose for the inexpressible — faces, tunes, tastes of wine — are the ones on which the separation happened. One might object that any learned system escapes Naur's rule pole. It does not, because the discriminator is not learning as such: a decision tree induced from examples is learned and is still an explicit rule set, inside Naur's rule-determined pole. The discriminator is whether the resulting judgment has a formulable rubric. A large language model reading a natural-language description of a design and judging whether a new demand resembles what the design already provides is on the same side of that line as the face recognizer. It is inside the machine pole, and it makes the kind of judgment Naur reserved for people.

Naur was right that the criteria cannot be formulated. What changed is that a machine no longer needs the criteria formulated in order to make the judgment.

None of this shows that any current system holds a program's theory. Separating execution from formulated criteria removes the argument that settles the question in advance; it does not answer it. Nor does it reopen the question of writing the theory down: what the compiler case shows is that propositions about a theory did not transfer possession of it, and that lesson is untouched. The open proposition is narrower — that text plus a sufficiently capable interpreter may transfer more of the capacity than text alone. What Naur's transfer cases still supply is a set of tests that any interpreter-plus-artifact composite must pass before it can claim to hold one. The composite must acquire *this* program's mapping, justification, and modification judgments, since general competence over language and the world is not the theory of this program. Some component must supply the [decision premises the interpreter cannot regenerate](../notes/design-rationale-must-preserve-unregenerable-decision-premises.md) from the implementation and general knowledge: which world affairs were ruled relevant and why, where the intended scope stops, which alternatives lost. And the capacity must be reliable across occasions, because one coherent extension may be luck.

## A self-witnessed case: the repair pass as the successor group

The claim that an interpreter-plus-text composite can hold a theory was tested, without anyone planning the test, on the note behind this article. It is evidence about one note on one day, and it is reported here because it is the article's claim run on the article's own machinery. The [basis note](../notes/naur-equates-machine-execution-with-formulated-criteria.md) went through two cycles of the project's automated improvement pass — a procedure that runs fixed gates over a note, records defeated premises, and repairs the note. Each cycle had the same shape. The author wrote a claim that overreached in a specific way. The pass found the overreach, correctly, with counterexamples that held: in the first cycle, that the note generalized from a subset of retained quotes to a source-wide claim; in the second, that it placed a trained interpreter "outside" Naur's partition when a language model is formal symbol manipulation on a computer and so inside it. Then the pass repaired by retreating to the nearest claim no gate could touch. The first cycle's claim, that Naur's human-only premise was *assumed*, became "Naur's retained passages do not establish a human-only theory bearer"; the second cycle's claim, that a trained interpreter escapes Naur's partition, became "the inexpressibility argument does not by itself bind program theory to humans." The pass recorded the contribution as preserved, then strengthened, and stopped. Both repairs were true. Neither was the claim the note was for: that the human binding rests on an identifiable premise, and what that premise is. What fixed the note each time was one sentence from the maintainer: first, that the human binding rests on a premise the pass had not looked for; second, that at the time, execution by machine meant formal interpretation, so the premise was accurate for its day and trained recognizers separated the two rather than escaping anything.

In Chris Argyris's terms ([source analysis](../sources/argyris-organizational-learning-and-mis-1977.ingest.md)) the pass corrected within its stated rules — single-loop learning, which fixes errors without questioning the policy that defines them — and its closing self-assessment was what he calls self-sealing: it measured the repaired note against a brief reconstructed from the text whose claim it had just defeated, and found the contribution strengthened. In Naur's terms the pass is the successor group. It had the note's text, every report, the collection contracts, and the gates, and its repairs were patches — adequate to the checks, framed outside the note's theory — because what the note was for was in none of its inputs. Critique did not need the theory: a counterexample to a universal, a source-wide claim without source-wide grounding, a misplaced system, are all visible from the text and the gates, and the pass found every one. Repair did. Of Naur's three tests, the episode fails the second: the decision premise the repair needed — what the note was for — was one the interpreter could not regenerate, and nothing in its inputs supplied it. The project's response was to [stop the pass at the point where repair would change a claim](../reference/adr/080-full-passes-hand-claim-changes-back-as-a-pending-revise.md) and hand the objections back to whoever holds the theory. That is Naur's own conclusion — transfer needs contact with the theory-holder — reached by the machinery the article is about, on the note the article is about.

## What would count as holding the theory

Naur's three tests should be pre-registered. Before claiming that an interpreter-plus-artifact composite holds a theory of a program, state what program-specific acquisition would look like, which decision premises the retained text must supply because the interpreter cannot regenerate them, and how many occasions of coherent modification count as reliability. The episode above failed the second test in a way that was visible only afterwards; pre-registering it would have made the failure a measurement.

## Where this leads

Separating execution from formulated criteria reopens a question Naur's argument had closed; it does not answer it, and the case above shows the composite failing one of his tests. What can be built on the reopened question — what makes retained theory operative rather than documentary, how an operative theory becomes learned state, and what formalization does and does not remove — is the subject of [*Theory building inside the system*](./theory-building-inside-the-system.md), which takes this article's conclusion as its premise.

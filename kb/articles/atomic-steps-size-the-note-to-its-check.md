# Atomic steps: size the note to its check

> **Draft.** This article is circulating for comments; its claims, structure, and central thesis may still change. Counterexamples, boundary cases, and disputed readings of the evidence are welcome through the repository's issue tracker.

A knowledge base written and reviewed by language-model agents has to decide how much evidence one review may read. In Commonplace the review that matters here is the grounding check: does the material a note cites support the claim it is cited for? That check recently had its reading budget raised from five linked artifacts to sixteen, after a paired experiment found four grounding failures that appeared only when the reviewer read past the fifth artifact. The decision sized the check to the notes — it measured how much evidence notes in the corpus offer and set the reviewer's budget high enough to cover most of them. This article argues for the reverse placement. Size the notes to the check: a note should be one *atomic step*, an inference whose supporting evidence a reviewer can read in a single pass, and a note that needs more sources than one step allows should quote them verbatim. A validated verbatim quote changes what the reviewer has to do, from finding support inside a source to judging a passage that a validator has already matched against the source. Atomic steps are not the same rule as atomic notes. Atomic notes bound a note to one claim so that many notes can be loaded together; atomic steps bound a note to one check so that its verdict can be trusted and redone. The two rules come apart on exactly the notes the experiment turned up: single-claim notes resting on six to nine sources. The article gives the case that motivates the idea, separates the two atomicities, says what a step is relative to, explains the verbatim escape hatch and its limits, argues why links to other notes do not count against a step, says what kind of number a step size is, and closes with what the idea does not decide.

## The case: four findings past the cap

Commonplace's grounding review is a language-model reviewer reading one note, following its links, and returning a verdict on whether each cited artifact supports the claim it is attached to. Until August 2026 the review instruction let the reviewer open at most five links. A [paired assay](../notes/evidence/a-five-link-cap-missed-four-grounding-findings-in-twelve-reviews.md) reviewed the same twelve notes under that instruction and under an otherwise identical instruction with the cap removed. Five verdicts differed. One difference reproduced when the capped review was repeated on the same three-artifact note, so it was reviewer noise. The other four appeared only in the uncapped arm, after the reviewer had opened six, eleven, fourteen, and sixteen distinct artifacts: two warnings and two failures. The uncapped reviewers read up to twenty-two artifacts and 322 kilobytes in one pass, and the three notes offering the most material — twenty-one to twenty-three artifacts — all passed.

Two readings fit these numbers, and they place the constraint on opposite sides of the review.

The first reading says the cap was too small. Four real findings sat between the sixth and sixteenth artifact; a reviewer that stops at five cannot reach them; so raise the cap to cover them. This is the reading the project adopted in [ADR 079](../reference/adr/079-grounding-reviews-budget-sixteen-distinct-linked-artifacts.md): the budget became sixteen distinct artifacts, chosen as the ninetieth percentile of what notes in the corpus offer and the largest count any observed miss needed. Reaching the budget is disclosure, not failure — the reviewer names what it left unread and scopes its verdict.

The second reading says the notes were larger than one check. The reviewer stopped at the cap, because that is what a cap does, and the notes had put their support beyond it. On this reading the four misses do not show that five is too few for a note with five sources; they show that a reviewer honours a reading limit, which is exactly the behaviour an artifact-side limit relies on. If the limit had been on the note — no more than N sources a reviewer must open — the four notes would have had to split or quote before review, and the reviewer would never have reached a cap on a conforming note.

The content of the four findings supports the second reading in a more specific way. The failure on the fourteen-artifact note was *missing retained support* for two details attributed to a source. The warning on the six-artifact note was that the note treated an ingest's analytical prose as evidence, beyond what its retained quotes contained. Both are absences of a retained passage, and an absence of that kind is a validator's job, not a reviewer's. Had the two details been quoted from the source, either the quote would exist and match, or the validator would have failed the note before any reviewer opened an artifact. The finding that sat at the fourteenth artifact was only ever going to be found by a reviewer that read fourteen artifacts because the note had asked the reviewer to do work that a quote would have discharged mechanically.

What the case establishes is this. A budget on the review has to be recalibrated whenever the corpus's evidence needs move, and the failure mode when it lags is silent: a reviewer that stops early and passes looks identical to one that read everything and passed. A budget on the artifact does not have that failure mode, because a conforming artifact fits by construction. The reviewer's limit exists, but it never binds. The rest of the article develops what "conforming" means.

## Two atomicities

Commonplace already has a rule that bounds note size, and it is easy to mistake this proposal for it. The existing rule is atomic notes: [one claim, one note](../notes/short-composable-notes-maximize-combinatorial-discovery.md), with the title stating the claim and the body supporting it. Its justification is co-loading. A model's context is bounded, so the number of notes that fit in it together determines how many independent perspectives can be juxtaposed, and juxtaposition is where cross-cutting structure is recognized. Short notes buy surface area for that recognition. The rule is inherited from Luhmann's Zettelkasten, but its reason here is the token limit, not filing convenience.

Atomic steps bound a note by a different quantity for a different reason. The quantity is the evidence a reviewer must read to check the note's grounding; the reason is the reviewer. A note is one step when the claim, the material it cites, and the inference from that material to the claim can be checked in a single pass by the check that is going to be applied to it. Where atomic notes are sized to the reader's context, atomic steps are sized to the checker's pass.

The two rules are independent, and the corpus shows it. Counting distinct source links per note across 344 notes, eight cite more than five sources, with a maximum of nine. Nearly all of them are single-claim notes — one proposition, established or bounded by reading six or more sources together. They are atomic notes by any reading of the rule. They are not atomic steps for the grounding check, because checking any one of them means opening six to nine artifacts and finding, in each, the support the note claims. The reverse case also exists in principle: a note making two independent claims, each grounded in one quoted passage, is one step for the grounding check and two notes by the atomic-note rule.

So the proposal adds a bound rather than restating one. Atomic notes answer "how much can a reader hold together?"; atomic steps answer "how much can a checker verify at once?". A knowledge base whose notes are consumed by agents and reviewed by agents needs both, because the two capacities are set by different mechanisms and, as the assay showed, the second one was the one that ran out.

## A step is relative to a check

"Fits one pass" has no meaning without saying whose pass. A step is atomic relative to a particular check — in the vocabulary this knowledge base uses for evaluation, relative to an [oracle and its domain](../notes/warranted-autonomy-is-bounded-by-oracle-domain.md): the range of candidates a checker can assess with the required confidence. The same note can be one step for one check and several for another. A note that quotes nine passages from nine sources is one step for the grounding check, since every passage is on the page and already matched; it may not be one step for a check that has to judge whether the inference across all nine holds.

This article states the rule for one check, the grounding check, because that is the check whose budget the assay was about and the one where the unit of cost is clear. The unit is a source the reviewer must open and search: an artifact that is not the note, in which the reviewer has to locate the passage that carries the note's claim and decide whether it does. The step size for the grounding check is therefore a bound on the number of such sources. Two kinds of link the note may carry do not have that cost, and the next two sections deal with them: a source the note has quoted, and a link to another note.

Stating the rule per check is not a weakness of the idea. It is what lets the number be defended. A step size chosen for the grounding check is a claim about what that check can do in one pass; a step size chosen for "review in general" would be a claim about nothing in particular.

## The escape hatch: quote, and the check changes form

A source that the note quotes verbatim does not count against the step. This is the clause that makes the rule affordable, and it works because of something the project has already built.

Commonplace validates every quotation marked `verbatim` against the file it links: the validator normalizes both texts and tests whether the quoted span occurs in the source, failing the note on a mismatch ([ADR 046](../reference/adr/046-verbatim-quotes-are-validated-against-their-cited-source.md)). Source material itself is held as retained quotes in a tracked ingest record, and each retained quote is in turn validated against the exact bytes of the captured source ([ADR 073](../reference/adr/073-untracked-source-snapshots-require-ingest-grounding.md)). A note that quotes a source therefore rests on two mechanical checks in sequence — the note's quote occurs in the ingest's retained quotes, and the retained quote occurs in the captured source — and neither involves a judgment.

What the reviewer then has to do is different in kind. Before the quote, the reviewer's task was to open the source, find the relevant passage, and judge whether it supports the claim; the finding step was the unbounded part, since a source has no title that says what it claims and the passage may be anywhere in 300 kilobytes. After the quote, the finding step is gone. The passage is on the page, its occurrence in the source is a validator's result, and the reviewer judges one thing: whether *this passage* supports *this claim*. That judgment still costs attention, and a note with nine quoted passages costs more than a note with one. But its cost is bounded by the author's choice of passage length, it involves no switching between artifacts, and the reviewer can do it without opening anything.

In the vocabulary of this knowledge base, the quote moves the support one step along the [representational-form](../notes/definitions/representational-form.md) axis: from natural-language material whose relation to the claim has to be judged, to a symbolic relation — string occurrence — that is decided by code, with a smaller natural-language judgment left over. That is the general pattern by which [a derived copy of recomputable truth](../notes/a-derived-copy-of-recomputable-truth-must-be-checked-or-absent.md) becomes safe to carry: a quote in the note is a copy of something held elsewhere, and a copy may exist only if a validator re-derives it from its source and fails on mismatch. A paraphrase cannot take this route. There is no mechanical test that a restatement occurs in a source, which is why the project rejected a paraphrased ledger as a grounding surface: checking a note against a paraphrase that was itself checked against the source is two semantic hops over one piece of evidence, and rewording at either hop can change the proposition. The escape hatch exists for the exact-copy layer alone, and [a citation cannot claim more fidelity than its capture preserved](../notes/a-citation-cannot-assert-more-fidelity-than-its-capture-preserved.md) — a passage that was never captured word for word cannot be quoted into the hatch later without returning to the source.

Two limits follow directly, and both are prices the project has already agreed to pay elsewhere.

The first is that occurrence is not representativeness. The validator establishes that the quoted words appear in the source. It does not establish that they mean, in context, what the note uses them for, and it cannot catch a quote that misleads by omission. That judgment stays with the reviewer, and a reviewer who suspects the passage has been lifted out of a qualifying context has to open the source after all. Commonplace's grounding gate already accepts this: on the quotes route the reviewer reads only the retained quotes as support, and if they are insufficient the review fails rather than falling back to the full source. The hatch does not add a new blind spot; it makes an existing one the author's responsibility, since the author chose the passage.

The second is that some claims cannot be quoted into a bounded passage at all. A claim that rests on the structure of a whole argument, on what a source does *not* say, or on material distributed across a document depends on the full source. Commonplace marks such uses `(snapshot required)` and the reviewer reads the captured source. A source cited that way has no bounded passage and counts fully against the step. Nor does the hatch bound bytes: a snapshot can be 750 kilobytes, and a step size stated in sources says nothing about how large each one is. An artifact-side rule stated in sources and a size exception for full-source reads are therefore complementary, not rivals.

There is one more consequence worth stating, because it answers the strongest objection to splitting notes. A claim supported *jointly* by several sources — none sufficient alone — cannot be split across notes without severing its support; each fragment would then fail a grounding check that the whole would pass. The project's [proposal for splitting over-budget reviews](../reference/proposals/exceeding-a-review-budget-splits-the-task.md) identified this hazard and concluded that any partition would have to be by claim, not by link, which requires the semantic judgment that was being budgeted in the first place. Under atomic steps the jointly supported claim is exactly the case for quoting rather than splitting. The author, who knows which passages jointly carry the claim, places them on the page together; the check reads them together; nothing is severed. The pack of evidence is assembled by the person who knows what is in it.

## Why links to other notes do not count

The rule counts sources and exempts links to other notes. That exemption is load-bearing — a five-source cap costs eight notes in the corpus, while a five-artifact cap counting notes and sources alike would violate more than half of it — so it needs an argument, not just a measurement. The argument is that a linked note and a cited source impose different obligations on the citing note, and only one of them is a grounding obligation.

A source is raw material. It makes no claim in the knowledge base's terms; the note that cites it asserts what it supports, and the grounding check exists to test that assertion. Nothing about the source bounds the work of testing it.

A linked note is a premise that carries its own certificate. Its [title states its claim](../notes/title-as-claim-enables-traversal-as-reasoning.md), so the citing note can say "since [claim]" and a reader can tell, from the title and a one-paragraph description, whether that is the claim being relied on. It has been through its own checks, including its own grounding check against its own sources; its grounding is its own step, discharged at its own review. What the citing note owes it is representation: that the claim invoked is the claim the linked note makes, at the scope the linked note makes it. That is a different, cheaper check — a title and a paragraph, not a search through a document — and Commonplace runs it as separate review criteria with their own limits on how many targets they open. So a linked note is not free; it is charged to a different step. Chaining notes this way is what it means for the graph to be an argument: each note is one checked inference, and a path through the graph is a proof in which every step has been checked once, near where it was made.

Why is this not the paraphrase-ledger mistake in different clothing? A paraphrase in a ledger *stands in for* a source: it exists to be checked instead of the source, over the same claim, so trusting it is a second semantic hop over one piece of evidence. A linked note does not stand in for its sources. It asserts a claim of its own, which its sources support at its own grounding review, and the citing note relies on the claim, not on the sources behind it. The two hops are over different propositions, and each is checked where it lives.

The exemption holds only while its preconditions do. Linked notes must actually pass their own grounding checks — an ungrounded note cited as a premise imports its debt. Titles must actually be claims, since a topic-titled note offers nothing a representation check can test against without opening it. And the representation check has to run; one of the four findings in the assay was a note attributing a broader mechanism to a linked artifact that explicitly disclaimed it, which is a representation failure and would not be caught by any count. Where these conditions fail, the exemption fails with them, and the honest reading is that the linked note has become a source.

## What kind of number the step size is

The proposal deliberately does not say what N is. It does say what kind of number N is, because three different kinds are on offer and they justify different values.

N is not the point at which reviewer performance degrades. The assay's uncapped reviewers read twenty-two artifacts and 322 kilobytes with no observed loss, and the notes offering the most material all passed. Whatever a small step protects, it is not a reviewer capacity that runs out at current sizes. A step size derived from a degradation curve would be large, and it would be the review-side number ADR 079 already chose.

N is not, either, the smallest count at which no miss was observed. That number moves with every assay and inherits the sample's selection.

N is a convention chosen so that a step is small enough for a second checker — including a person — to redo. This is what a small step buys that a well-calibrated reviewer budget never does. When the reviewer's verdict on a note rests on fourteen artifacts, checking the verdict costs what the verdict cost, and in practice the verdict is trusted. When it rests on three sources and six quoted passages, a maintainer can look at the same page and see whether the reviewer was right. The value is not that the reviewer copes; it is that the review becomes auditable, and an auditable review is one whose errors can be found. The project's own evidence that language-model verdicts disagree with themselves — one of five repeated capped reviews returned a different outcome on an unchanged note — is the reason auditability is worth paying for.

This is the sense in which the idea is old. Descartes' second precept of method was "to divide each of the difficulties under examination into as many parts as possible, and as might be necessary for its adequate solution", and the reason he gave was what such division buys: "The long chains of simple and easy reasonings by means of which geometers are accustomed to reach the conclusions of their most difficult demonstrations" had persuaded him that anything within reach of knowledge could be reached "provided only we abstain from accepting the false for the true, and always preserve in our thoughts the order necessary for the deduction of one truth from another" ([*Discourse on the Method*, Part II](../sources/descartes-discourse-on-the-method.ingest.md), verbatim, Veitch's translation). The parts are sized to the solution, and the chain is trusted because each link is simple enough to be evident.

Lamport's case for hierarchically structured proofs makes the checker explicit. Asked how much detail a proof step needs, he answers that when the proof is written to convince someone else "the answer depends on the sophistication of the reader. A beginning student needs more help understanding a proof than does a mathematician", and when it is written for oneself, "if the truth of a statement is not completely obvious, or if you suspect that there may be just the slightest possibility that it is not correct, then more detail is needed" ([*How to Write a 21st Century Proof*, section 3](../sources/lamport-how-to-write-a-21st-century-proof.ingest.md), verbatim). Step size is set relative to whoever checks the step, which is the claim made above about oracles in different words. Lamport also reports what the structure is for. His earlier paper "recounted how only by writing such a proof was I able to re-discover an error in a proof of the Schroeder-Bernstein theorem in a well-known topology text", and his assessment of the method is measured: "Eliminating errors requires care. Structured proofs make it possible, not inevitable" ([section 5](../sources/lamport-how-to-write-a-21st-century-proof.ingest.md), verbatim). That is the claim this article makes for small steps — not that a bounded check catches every error, but that an unbounded one cannot be redone. The knowledge base applies the same convention to a note and its evidence, with one addition the earlier cases did not have: a validator that turns the most common kind of step — this passage says this — into a check that costs nothing.

## What this does not decide

The article has argued that a cap belongs on the artifact, that its unit for the grounding check is the unquoted source, that verbatim quotes discharge a source mechanically, and that linked notes are charged to a different check. It has not decided the number, the validator rule that would enforce it, or what becomes of the review-side budget in ADR 079, which stands as written. Those are operational decisions with their own evidence requirements, and the article should be read as the argument they would rest on rather than as a proposal to make them.

It also leaves open a question the argument raises rather than settles: whether the representation check on linked notes — the one the exemption relies on — is itself well bounded, or whether it is the next place the same problem appears in a smaller form. The assay's misattribution finding suggests that check has real work to do, and nothing in this article has sized it.

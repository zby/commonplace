# From controlled HCL benchmarks to deployment-time learning

> **Status:** This Commonplace workshop reading separates what Harness Continual Learning (HCL) tests in controlled benchmark streams from the target setting we want to investigate: a system that learns from evidence produced by real-world tasks. It proposes a transfer of techniques, not a reclassification of HCL's experiments. The paired [source analysis](../../sources/harness-continual-learning-adaptation-beyond-model-parameters.ingest.md) records the evidence and qualifications in more detail.

## The transfer thesis

Our target is a deployed system that learns from real-world task execution. Outcomes, user or operator feedback, observed failures, and later consequences can supply evidence for proposing a change. Reject-capable evaluation must then select the change, and retained behavior-shaping state must affect later tasks. The task result is therefore a training signal, not an instruction to mutate the system directly. An unevaluated manual patch remains durable maintenance rather than learning. The relevant learning unit is [the deployed system, not the model alone](../../notes/the-deployed-system-not-the-model-is-the-unit-of-learning.md).

HCL operationalizes a closely related proposal–evaluation–retention loop under controlled conditions. Evidence from a benchmark task stream drives its optimizer, its evaluator can block adoption, and atomic commitment retains revised interface, memory, capability, and routing state for later tasks. These are candidate techniques for governing real-world system learning. Our thesis is that they may transfer to that target setting, not that HCL itself is deployment-time learning or that its results establish the transfer. The underlying structure is described in [A proposal-selection improvement loop requires search, evaluation, and operative retention](../../notes/a-proposal-selection-loop-requires-search-evaluation-and-retention.md).

## Techniques and evidence HCL supplies

HCL fixes foundation-model parameters and jointly versions four parts of persistent harness state: the task interface, experience memory, capability map, and adaptive router. Its optimizer constructs an isolated candidate. When several parts can change, search proceeds through a predefined component order while the other parts stay fixed. The final transition is still all-or-none. Joint versioning and atomic commitment do not make candidate construction a joint optimization process.

The evaluator admits a candidate only when current-task improvement, historical retention, and validity all hold. Historical loss `D_n` counts a finite sample of earlier-task checks, or anchors, that the incumbent solves and the candidate newly fails. The acceptance gate requires `D_n` to remain within the permitted loss budget `B_n`. When `B_n = 0`, every incumbent-solved anchor in that sample is protected. Unsampled behavior lies outside the count, and a previously failed anchor does not count as a new loss. Even with strict sampled-anchor protection, several reported controlled regimes still showed nonzero held-out forgetting. A zero budget therefore tightens acceptance over the observed checks; it does not enlarge the set of behaviors for which the evidence warrants a no-regression conclusion. [Warranted autonomy is bounded by oracle domain](../../notes/warranted-autonomy-is-bounded-by-oracle-domain.md) develops this evidence-domain distinction.

HCL's independent textual budget sweep also exposes an evaluation-cost surface worth measuring. Under the maximal-check assumption used in this workshop, its schedule yields `10 * 80 * (0 + 1 + 2 + 3) = 4,800` proposal–historical-anchor case checks per profile. This is a conditional case count, not measured runtime cost or evidence that selective evaluation is the right optimization: the paper does not report the effects of caching, batching, or short-circuiting.

HCL therefore supplies a costly proposal–evaluation–retention loop, but not a general account of how one candidate can affect the rest of the system. This workshop asks whether a theory with explanatory-reach could serve as an evaluation prior: derive a candidate-specific impact projection, turn that projection into evaluation obligations, and spend evidence where those obligations make it decision-relevant. An explicit causal model is one route because the query concerns a proposed intervention. Dependency and authority structure, invariants, programs, semantic theories, and learned action-conditioned predictors may support other or mixed routes.

## What does not transfer automatically

HCL does not establish that benchmark-task evidence is an adequate substitute for evidence from real-world work. In deployment, outcomes may be delayed, noisy, incomplete, or shaped by people and environments outside the evaluator's boundary. The task history may also be longer and less stationary than a controlled stream. A deployment-time system would need to define which observations count as training evidence, which effects its evaluator can warrant, and how candidate isolation and rollback work in the live system. These are requirements of the proposed transfer, not claims that the HCL paper was obliged to test.

HCL's four parts are an engineering partition over mutable state. Its update-permission ablations test bundles of allowed persistent updates; they do not establish those four parts as necessary components, as the right explanatory decomposition, as invariant modules or intervention variables, or as boundaries within which behavioral effects remain local. [An experiment identifies only the contrast it actually runs](../../notes/an-experiment-identifies-only-the-contrast-it-actually-runs.md) provides this treatment-grain limit.

This workshop therefore does not treat HCL's partition as a validated theory of system operation or, more specifically, as a validated causal graph.

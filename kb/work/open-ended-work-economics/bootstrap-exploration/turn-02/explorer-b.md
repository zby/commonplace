# Explorer B: stop an episode without closing the possibility

## Finding and decision boundary

**Productive drift need not produce immediate usefulness, but it must remain interruptible without redefining every interruption as premature.** The useful distinction is not exploration versus delivery. It is exploration that exposes its assumptions to possible defeat versus preparation that continually moves the conditions under which those assumptions could be tested.

This is a conceptual finding and a proposed control principle, not a demonstrated performance result. Preserve both useful knowledge work and learning to automate methodologies. The [turn-1 integration](../turn-01/integration.md) leaves their relative priority unresolved. Permission to finish five turns removes response gates; it does not authorize an indefinite research budget or answer that priority question.

My recommendation is to separate three decisions:

1. May this episode explore outside its starting question? Here, yes.
2. Has the episode earned another allocation? Novel possibilities are relevant but not sufficient evidence.
3. Should a discovered possibility replace the purpose? Only a separately authorized purpose decision can do that.

The next-question proposal from turn 1 is warranted: ask what observation would change continuation, redirection or stopping. But requiring every exploratory step to name its eventual consumer would select against the drift this commission explicitly permits. The proposal below places a boundary around the episode, not a demand for immediate application on each step.

## 1. Discovery progress can precede usefulness without becoming immune to criticism

An initial framing restricts what an investigator looks for. Exploration can be valuable by discovering that a supposedly necessary dependency is unnecessary, that an existing criterion rewards the wrong result, or that a neglected distinction changes which experiment would be informative. None requires a ready downstream consumer.

A conjectural example within this commission: preparing a large method-extraction process might expose that the apparent difficulty lies in recognizing when not to use a method. That changes the investigation from producing more reusable instructions to identifying applicability failures. The bootstrap purpose survives; the proposed means changes. The discovery does not yet establish that applicability checks work or deserve implementation.

For reporting, distinguish:

- **New possibility:** a previously unconsidered route is now expressible. This earns attention, not automatic renewal.
- **Changed understanding:** a concrete assumption has been withdrawn, narrowed or replaced, with a reason that survives a serious rival.
- **Changed investigation:** the next comparison, evidence source or stop condition changes because of that understanding.
- **Observed benefit:** later work improves because the change was retained or used.

These are claim distinctions, not mandatory stages or a scoring scheme. A negative investigation can end at changed understanding. A useful one-off exploration need not become an automated methodology. Conversely, rewriting the question in more sophisticated language is not necessarily changed understanding.

The preparation failure arises when each discovery is used only to add another prerequisite before any contrary observation can matter. A dependency chain can be real. Its length alone does not establish waste. The suspicious feature is **one-way revision**: favorable results authorize continuation, unfavorable results demand more preparation, and missing results justify more time. No conceivable near-term observation can release resources.

This diagnostic concerns the work's response structure, not an agent's psychology. “Self-protecting” here means that the process protects continuation from criticism; it does not attribute fear or intent to a model.

## 2. Keep discovery freedom inside a boundary the discovery cannot silently move

A workable conjecture is that an exploratory episode can have a fixed authorization boundary while its intellectual target remains revisable. The boundary may be a turn, a time allocation, a bounded source search or a named deliverable. Which boundary is appropriate depends on the work; none is universally optimal.

Inside it, a surprising result can redirect reading without proving immediate usefulness. At its end, the report should distinguish what changed from what merely became interesting. Renewal must compete with alternatives rather than follow automatically from unfinished ideas.

This resolves part of the apparent conflict with productive deferral. An episode whose purpose is to discover options need not begin with a known option to preserve. Once an investigator asks to postpone a consequential commitment because of a newly found option, the deferral test becomes relevant: is the option actually exercisable, can more evidence alter the choice, and when does that choice return?

A bounded episode can therefore end with unresolved promise. Stopping does not assert that the branch is worthless. It asserts that this allocation is over or that no further allocation is currently justified. Resumption can depend on a new observation, changed demand, available evidence or an explicit later allocation. Merely retaining an idea does not reserve resources for it.

The existing full-improvement closure rule is an analogy at this boundary only: remaining findings do not force another cycle. Open exploration lacks that workflow's stable artifact and assay set, so copying its exact closing procedure would be unwarranted.

## 3. Preparation should expose a necessity claim, not merely an attractive improvement

When work is described as preparation, a claim is often hidden: *without this work, the intended attempt would fail, be unsafe or teach us too little*. That claim is stronger than *this preparation might improve the attempt*.

Consider a hypothetical proposal to build a general episode ledger before testing whether a method reminder helps a second task. A small comparison may require only a retained reminder, an explicit learner boundary and enough outcome evidence to avoid guessing. If so, the general ledger is not a prerequisite. It is a competing investment. This does not reject the ledger or license a deficient test; it makes its opportunity cost visible.

The [proximate-target argument](../../../../theory/a-proximate-target-is-checked-for-achievement-not-for-warrant.md) explains the danger: checking that a desired intermediate property exists does not test whether it serves the purpose. Here, “the system is ready to learn” can become an intermediate target whose achievement is repeatedly refined while actual learning remains unobserved.

A useful challenge is therefore: **what would become possible after this preparation that cannot responsibly be attempted in a smaller form now?** A concrete answer can justify preparation. Failure to answer does not prove worthlessness, but weakens a claim of necessity. It may be better described as research in its own right, then judged as such rather than hidden inside delivery overhead.

There is an important exception: some prerequisites protect safety, authority or evidential validity. Removing them to obtain an early result can invalidate the result or violate the commission. “Try the smaller attempt” is not a universal command.

## 4. Opportunity cost need not be monetized, but it needs an actual alternative

“More research costs something” does little decision work. A stronger comparison identifies what another allocation would displace: finishing a useful task, trying the simplest method on a second case, investigating the strongest objection, or doing no additional work until relevant evidence exists.

No common numerical scale is available here. Operator attention, model expenditure and delayed delivery cannot honestly be collapsed into a fabricated score. A qualitative comparison can still expose dominance: if the smaller attempt addresses the same uncertainty without the proposed infrastructure, extra infrastructure needs a separate justification. But even that claim requires checking that the smaller attempt preserves the relevant conditions.

Repeated small renewals also need to be understood as cumulative expenditure. Individually bounded episodes can collectively become endless preparation when every boundary resets the question to “is one more small step interesting?” A renewal should consider what the line of inquiry has already displaced, not treat sunk expenditure as a reason to continue. Accumulated cost matters for diagnosing the strategy; irrecoverable cost is not itself a prospective benefit.

For this trial, the authorized five turns establish a boundary. They do not establish an acceptable ongoing allocation after turn five. No evidence in my reading fixes that allocation, and I do not infer it.

## Strongest rival: protected exploration, then ordinary judgment

The strongest rival is simpler than a formal continuation controller: give exploration a protected finite allocation, allow undirected reading and thought, and have a capable operator judge what emerged. Requiring explicit changed decisions at every renewal may favor easily verbalized, locally legible progress. It may reject the unfamiliar possibility whose importance cannot yet be articulated. Worse, a language model may cheaply manufacture plausible “decision changes” to satisfy the rule.

This rival preserves the exploratory purpose and deserves a fair comparison. My recommendation already concedes much of it: no per-step utility test, no compulsory ledger and no numerical progress score. The remaining conjectural advantage is at renewal: making a displaced alternative and a defeasible reason explicit may prevent automatic continuation and prerequisite inflation.

A possible bounded comparison, not an executed or commissioned protocol, would give the same research question and comparable allocations to ordinary bounded exploration and exploration with an explicit renewal challenge. The relevant outcomes would include whether unfamiliar useful possibilities survive, whether preparation dependencies are actually needed, and whether the resulting evidence changes a later investigation. Counting output volume, declared surprises or compliance with the renewal form would not discriminate the accounts. This would test the renewal intervention, not the whole bootstrap strategy. Reliable assessment of unfamiliar possibilities remains difficult.

## Possible changed decisions and preserved conflicts

1. **Stop demanding an immediate application from each drift.** Ask for the claim the episode actually supports; do not inflate discovery into service benefit.
2. **Treat preparation as a proposed investment unless its necessity is defended.** This could move a minimal use or comparison ahead of a general methodology system. It does not mandate service-first allocation.
3. **Separate returning control from rejecting the idea.** A parked possibility can remain intellectually live without remaining an active resource commitment.
4. **Do not let discovery alone renew its own budget.** Within this trial, the coordinator owns scheduling. Beyond it, renewal needs an authorized allocation decision; the operator has not delegated a universal research-versus-service preference.
5. **Retain the conflict over delayed relevance.** Changed later decisions are stronger evidence than interesting output, but absence of a present changed decision does not refute the value of exploratory work. It leaves realized value unestablished.

Restricting all exploration to named consumers, adopting autonomous indefinite research, or replacing the bootstrap with task-only assistance would be consequential purpose changes. None is adopted here.

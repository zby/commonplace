# Turn 2 integration: allow question changes without automatic renewal

## Outcome

**Keep exploration free to revise its starting question within an authorized allocation. Do not treat that freedom, a changed question, or orderly closure as evidence that the exploration was productive.** The strongest surviving distinction is between discovering a possibility, deciding to investigate it further, and changing the purpose. Those are separate decisions.

[Explorer A](./explorer-a.md) supplies a reason not to judge drift only by its original target: an inspected case can challenge the categories in which the target was expressed. [Explorer B](./explorer-b.md) supplies a reason not to let every discovery generate another prerequisite: an episode must be able to end with unresolved possibilities. The [critic](./critic.md) identifies the shared limit: a succession of authorized, finite episodes can still be endless preparation. Returning control protects authority; it does not solve selection.

The purpose remains useful knowledge work and learning to automate methodologies. The operator's instruction to complete all five turns removes response gates, not the unresolved choice between present service and speculative learning investment. This integration introduces no gate, standing research entitlement, controller, or doctrine change.

## What changed in the account

### 1. Preserve two assessments when exploration leaves its target

A's inspection of the curiosity experiment record distinguishes attainment of a preselected finding from incidental findings reported alongside it. The critic checked that documentary distinction. This is evidence about an evaluation record's organization, not independent evidence that the incidental findings were valuable or that a particular prompt improves discovery.

The useful revision is to keep original-task completion and unexpected findings separately assessable. A missed target stays missed. An unexpected result can deserve narrower research credit without retroactively becoming the original acceptance condition. Conversely, meeting the target need not exhaust what the episode discovered.

This avoids two opposite errors: rejecting a genuine challenge because it is off-target, and calling any deviation success after seeing the result. It does not supply an evaluator capable of distinguishing valuable unfamiliar possibilities from attractive distractions.

### 2. Challenge prerequisites without making minimal preparation the objective

B asks what a proposed preparation makes possible that cannot responsibly be attempted in a smaller form now. That question can expose a general ledger being treated as a prerequisite for a small reuse comparison. The example is hypothetical; no unnecessary prerequisite was removed in this turn.

The critic's qualification changes the recommendation: **require a necessity argument only when necessity is asserted.** Preparation can be worthwhile because it improves measurement, reduces repair, or makes a result less misleading, even when a smaller attempt is feasible. Compare the uncertainty each alternative can resolve and its burden. No evidence here establishes that the smallest attempt is best or cheaper overall.

A preparation sequence becomes suspect when every outcome supports continuation: success warrants expansion, failure requires more infrastructure, and absence of results requires more time. This is a proposed diagnostic, not a demonstrated classifier of waste. Real prerequisites can be long; safety, authority and evidential validity cannot be dropped to obtain a quick result.

### 3. End allocations without declaring possibilities worthless

Both explorers borrow the distinction between returning to a decision and eliminating uncertainty. An episode can end with a counterexample, a narrowed claim, an unanswered question or a reason not to continue. Retention of a possibility does not reserve resources for it.

The closure analogy in B's report is narrow: a local improvement workflow can stop with residual findings. It does not establish a stopping policy for open exploration. Likewise, the productive-deferral argument concerns a consequential choice already being postponed; applying its full test before an unfamiliar possibility has even been encountered would demand the discovery before permitting the search.

This distinction is already present in adjacent KB work. Applying it here is useful, but explorer agreement is not independent confirmation or a new performance result.

## Surviving conflicts

**Promising output versus demonstrated benefit.** The explorers resist interpreting a discovery without a later consumer as worthless. The existing [controller-evaluation argument](../../../../theory/a-search-controller-is-tested-by-what-it-brings-to-stronger-evaluation.md) emphasizes later consequences and changed decisions. These can be compatible if prospective promise is kept separate from realized controller benefit. But the amount of unresolved promise worth financing remains unsettled. Renaming an output does not answer the allocation question.

**Freedom versus legibility.** Requiring an explicit changed assumption at every step could exclude unfamiliar ideas whose significance is not yet expressible. Requiring no evidence at renewal could reward fluent accounts of progress indefinitely. Initial permission to inspect an unfamiliar case and justification for another allocation need not use the same standard. This is a plausible arrangement, not a tested optimum.

**Reframing versus forgetting.** A claimed surprise may merely exaggerate the old plan's commitments. Applicability failures, for example, were already part of turn 1's adaptation distinction. Before crediting a premise reversal, check that the plan actually relied on the premise, that inspected evidence challenges it, and that a serious alternative interpretation has been considered. A genuinely new question may still be less useful than the old one.

**Bounded execution versus productive selection.** Five finite turns can comply fully with the commission while adding little. A coordinator can repeatedly renew weak work. Cumulative expenditure therefore matters to evaluating the strategy, although sunk cost is not a reason to continue. This turn does not identify a reliable automatic selector or an acceptable ongoing research budget.

## Actual dispositions and unadopted proposals

The changes made here are interpretive decisions for this integration and its next-question recommendation, not implemented operating policy:

| Disposition | Effect on the investigation |
|---|---|
| Assess the original target and incidental findings separately | Do not infer success or failure of the entire exploration from either alone |
| Narrow B's preparation challenge | Treat necessity and worthwhile improvement as different claims |
| Accept closure without intellectual rejection | Do not turn parked possibilities into an automatic continuation queue |
| Reject boundedness as proof of productivity | Keep repeated authorized preparation as the strongest unresolved failure case |
| Select a concrete retention question for the next turn | Do not move directly from these conceptual distinctions to a general record-system design |

A proposes comparing work with and without a separately bounded exploration opportunity. B proposes comparing ordinary bounded exploration with exploration that adds an explicit renewal challenge. These are different interventions. The first concerns reserving exploration effort; the second concerns adding allocation discipline. Neither comparison was run or commissioned here. Combining them would obscure which change caused any result. Fixed total resources would make exploration displace other work; extra resources would require accounting for the extra effort.

The strongest simple rival remains **protected finite exploration followed by ordinary judgment and concise observations**. It can notice surprises, reject distractions and stop. Task-first assistance remains another relevant investment alternative, not an adopted replacement purpose. A specialized renewal process must improve on competent rivals rather than on an agent that follows every interesting idea indefinitely.

Within-purpose changes include improving refusal, question clarification or applicability judgment when these serve useful work and the investigation of methodology automation. Purpose changes would include restricting all research to named service consumers, abandoning the bootstrap for task-only assistance, making operator understanding the universal product, or authorizing indefinite autonomous research. None is adopted. Relative priority remains reserved where it determines acceptance.

## Proposed next question

**For one concrete surprising case, what must a later worker recover to apply, revise or decline the resulting method, and what—if anything—does an ordinary concise correction fail to provide?**

This follows A's retention question with the critic's correction: retention must not be presumed beneficial. It also follows turn 1's distinction between useful delivery, learning investment and later benefit. The question serves the executable-methodology direction without assuming every discovery should become an instruction.

A bounded answer could identify missing information, show that a concise correction suffices, or find that the proposed method is not ready for reuse. None would justify a general store by default. This is a recommendation to the coordinator, not a launched turn, required experiment or operator response gate.

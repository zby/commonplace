# Learning follows when experience invalidates the held theory

The first document derived that a software factory system which keeps
changing coherently must hold a theory in Naur's meaning. Learning enters
that account in two distinct places: acquiring a theory and revising one
that no longer fits. Naur supplies the first; the second follows only under
an additional condition about what production experience reveals.

**Acquisition is participation, not document transfer.** Naur's later
compiler group had the program and extensive documentation, yet proposed
changes that cut across the design. His positive account is that a new holder
builds the theory by doing relevant programming work in close contact with
people who already hold it, under their guidance and in discussion of the
program's relation to the world. The claim is not that work alone suffices,
nor that production experience is the theory's only possible input. It is
that situated, guided participation teaches what reading the documented
products did not convey to those programmers.

**Program modification is not necessarily theory modification.** Naur says
that, in a certain sense, there can be no question of theory modification,
only program modification: having the theory means being prepared to relate
new demands to the existing organization. A theory with explanatory reach
can govern many changes without itself changing. Novelty alone therefore
does not imply learning.

**Experience can invalidate the held theory.** Production can nevertheless
expose that the current mapping, organizational account, or way of relating
demands no longer fits: a delayed consequence contradicts the account, a
purpose changes, or a demand falls outside distinctions the theory can
extend. If the system is to keep changing coherently after such an episode,
its theory-holding capability must change in response and the change must
persist into later work. That is learning: production experience causing a
retained change to what the system holds.

So a software factory system can exist, and can make theory-governed program
modifications, without learning. It must learn when its production
experience exposes that the held theory is no longer adequate and coherent
change must continue. Whether economically relevant scopes produce such
episodes often enough to make learning a standing requirement is an
empirical premise of the program, not a result Naur establishes.

## What this means per realization

For people, acquisition is guided participation and revision is a changed
understanding retained in the holders. For codified assets, changing what
they carry means re-codification — the cost that bound the first wave. For
retained readable artifacts, it means revising the cached theory-building
results and having the composite hold the revised theory — cheap in
principle, and exactly the holding-and-revision machinery the previous
document conjectures is not yet there. For weights, it means retraining.

## What would disarm it

A system may hold a fitting theory across a long, novelty-bringing horizon
without revising it: that demonstrates the theory's reach, not a failure to
learn. The operative premise is disarmed where novelty never exposes an
inadequacy in the held theory. It gains force only where production supplies
such episodes and the system must retain a changed theory-holding capability
to continue coherently.

## What this does not yet give

Whether a particular revision made the theory or the system better, how
that would be measured, and who or what gets credit for the learning —
quality, measurement, and attribution — belong to the testing layer, not to
the derivation.

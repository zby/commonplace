The settled part, exactly specified structure, is what the [commitment account](./codification-as-commitment.md) uses to gloss "delimited" in its hypothesis, and step 2 depends on nothing else here. ## Settled: exactly specified structure

Tarski says the problem of truth has a precise meaning only for languages whose structure has been exactly specified: which expressions are meaningful, which terms are undefined, what the rules of definition are, which expressions are sentences, and under what conditions a sentence may be asserted. For natural, spoken languages the problem is vague and its solution can only be approximate. This is the precondition the clerk conjunct of the [commitment account](./codification-as-commitment.md) assumes: a reference a clerk could follow presupposes a language whose sentences and assertion conditions a clerk could recognize. Tarski states it as a precondition, not a result.

## Open: semantic closure and the metalanguage

Three properties must be kept apart here, because the first draft of this thread ran them together. *Formalizability* is exactly specified structure; arithmetic and programming languages have it. *Undefinability* is Tarski's theorem: a consistent exactly specified language that can name its own sentences cannot define its own truth predicate, so truth for it is defined in an essentially richer metalanguage. Arithmetic has this property, and it is a consequence of being formalized and consistent, not a failure of formalization. *The antinomy* affects only a language that is exactly specified, classical, fully disquotational and semantically closed, that is, one that contains its own truth predicate for its own sentences. Arithmetic is not semantically closed, which is why it is consistent. Everyday language appears to be, which is why Tarski guesses that an exactly specified language resembling it would be inconsistent.

The ingest's limits on what this gives the workshop follow. Undefinability holds for arithmetic and for programming languages, so it cannot be what separates them from natural language. The 1944 paper never mentions computation or execution. The defensible core is narrower: natural language cannot be formalized while keeping universality, classical logic and full disquotation together, so one of them must go, and every formalization of a fragment drops universality. A further question the ingest raises for the thread: a programming language can contain its own interpreter, as Lisp does, without contradiction, because an evaluator fixes execution rather than truth and a partial function has no liar. Whether "operationally closed but not semantically closed" is the precise form of the programming-language side is open.

## Universality, in Tarski's own words

The claim that the common language is universal was in the 1933 monograph, whose scans are poor. Tarski's 1969 article [Truth and Proof](../../sources/tarski-truth-and-proof.ingest.md) states it in clean text: "The common language is universal and is intended to be so", and it is "semantically universal" in a sense he defines there, naming its own expressions and containing semantic terms for them. Two differences from 1944 matter. "Semantically universal" does not require that every instance of the truth schema be assertible, which "semantically closed" does, so the 1944 paper remains the text for the classical-logic and full-disquotation assumptions, which the 1969 article leaves unstated. And fragments are not a second category beside formalized languages: the only formalized languages of real interest are fragments of natural languages provided with complete vocabularies and precise syntactical rules, and separately the rules should be purely formal, meaning depending on form alone, so "this" and "here" are excluded.

The structural conjecture now rests on two quotable steps. First, the liar compelled Tarski to abandon a definition of truth for natural languages and restrict it to formalized languages. Second, within those, a metalanguage that cannot be translated into the object language keeps the object language from becoming semantically universal and so blocks the reconstruction of the liar; the languages of the sciences, logic and linguistics do not have to be semantically universal. The conjecture therefore reads: a programming language's reference is stated in a metalanguage the language cannot absorb, while the common language absorbs any metalanguage, so a reference for it can stand outside it only as a machine adopted by stipulation or as a restriction to a fragment with purely formal rules. The redefinition feature in the [tentative theory](./where-meaning-is-settled.md), that the semantic vocabulary is inside the language, is this universality seen from the speaker's side.

Two limits. Tarski's claims about natural language stay hedged ("seems to preclude"). And the [encyclopedia entry on the liar](../../sources/iep-liar-paradox.ingest.md) says in its own voice, not Tarski's, that reflection on the liar suggests English is either not semantically closed or inconsistent, hedged on whether "inconsistent" applies to a language with vague structure; it raises without resolving whether English is semantically closed, and reports significant disagreement on how formal-language solutions relate to natural-language ones. Kripke's partial truth predicate is the main alternative it presents. The conjecture is grounded as Tarski's view, not as a settled result. "Semantic closure" also has an unrelated biological sense in the KB's Pattee ingests, so text using the term should say which sense it means.

## The library audit

For everyday language Tarski says the consistency question "has no exact meaning", and he only risks the guess that an exactly specified language resembling it would be inconsistent. The library's note on [underspecified instructions](../../theory/agentic-systems-interpret-underspecified-instructions.md) currently appeals to "Tarski, Gödel and halting-style impossibility results" together, and the [institution-theory workshop](../institution-theory-import/import-assessment.md) flagged that appeal for this audit. The ingest's assessment is that the appeal can draw the hierarchy of languages and the absence of a general truth test, and cannot draw a theorem about natural language. The 1969 article sharpens this: truth exceeding proof holds for any theory containing arithmetic, with elementary geometry and real algebra as named exceptions, so the dividing line is a theory's expressive strength, not natural versus formal language. Neither Tarski text mentions computation or halting. The encyclopedia entry adds that the undefinability theorem does not apply to languages with truth-value gaps, and, with a hedge, that Kripke's language can then contain its own global truth predicate. The 1944 paper requires an essentially richer metalanguage only for defining truth; it allows truth as an undefined term with axioms otherwise. That paragraph should be rewritten to the narrower claim once this thread settles, citing the three ingests, with the passages they name retained as quotes.

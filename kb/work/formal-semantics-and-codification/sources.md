## Sources

- [Schegloff, Jefferson and Sacks, "The preference for self-correction in the organization of repair in conversation" (1977)](../../sources/schegloff-jefferson-sacks-self-correction-repair.ingest.md): repair is a structural feature of conversation with its own organization, not a failure mode. Ingested, seven quotes retained. The paper supports that claim: repair occurs where nothing is hearably wrong, errors often go unrepaired, nothing is in principle excluded from the repairable, and the authors call repair the self-righting mechanism of language use in interaction. It does not separate what an expression conventionally means from what the speaker intended, and it ties repair to continuing the next action. The KB uses "self-repair" for an LLM regenerating code until a check passes; the paper's sense is repair by the speaker, so the collision ledger may need an entry. The opportunity-space passage is in section 4.2, not 4.3 as the ingest first said.
- [Purver, Ginzburg and Healey, "On the means for clarification in dialogue" (2001)](../../sources/purver-ginzburg-healey-clarification-dialogue.ingest.md): clarification requests are frequent in ordinary dialogue and have classifiable forms and readings. Open stand-in for Ginzburg's *The Interactive Stance* (2012), which is not freely available. Ingested. It supports frequency and classifiability only: requests are about 3 to 4 percent of dialogue sentences in the British National Corpus, with eight forms and four readings, and 96 percent target a sentence at most four back. Only about 15 percent ask what an expression refers to; most ask whether the words were heard or what move the speaker made. So the repair feature above must not be read as showing that meaning in general is settled by asking. Single annotator, no agreement check. Seven quotes retained; cite the constituent share as "about 15%".
- [Tarski, "The semantic conception of truth and the foundations of semantics" (1944)](../../sources/tarski-semantic-conception-of-truth.ingest.md): exactly specified structure as the precondition for a precise semantics (section 6); the semantically closed language and the decision not to use one (section 8); truth defined in an essentially richer metalanguage (section 10). Ingested, whole paper captured. Universality of colloquial language is not in this paper; it is in the 1933 monograph, which is not ingested. Undefinability of a language's own truth holds for formalized arithmetic too, and the paper says nothing about computation, so neither separates natural from programming languages; the antinomy itself hits only semantically closed languages, which arithmetic is not. Eight quotes retained. Essential richness is required only for defining truth; Tarski allows truth as an undefined term with axioms otherwise.
- [Tarski, "Truth and Proof" (1969)](../../sources/tarski-truth-and-proof.ingest.md): the common language is universal and semantically universal in a defined sense; the languages of the sciences need not be; the only formalized languages of real interest are fragments of natural languages with complete vocabularies and purely formal rules; the liar compelled the restriction to formalized languages, and a metalanguage the object language cannot absorb blocks its reconstruction. Ingested in place of the 1933 monograph, whose scans are poor. The copy captured is a university course-site PDF; the official source is Scientific American 220(6), and the copy's authorization is unverified. Its natural-language claims are hedged. Six quotes retained, kept with the snapshot's line breaks and OCR errors because the quote checker matches bytes. "Complete vocabularies and precise syntactical rules" and "purely formal" are two passages on p. 68, not one.
- [Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Liar paradox" (Dowden)](../../sources/iep-liar-paradox.ingest.md): the definition of a semantically closed language, quoted from Tarski 1944; the reading that natural language is either not semantically closed or inconsistent, which the entry gives in its own voice and marks as disputed; Kripke's partial truth predicate, with the hedged claim that undefinability does not apply to languages with gaps. Secondary conceptual essay, so it bounds the thread's claims rather than corroborating them. Quotes retained for all four points. The entry quotes Tarski's definition rather than giving its own; it raises without resolving whether English is semantically closed; the "does not apply" sentence is unhedged and the hedge sits on the global-truth-predicate claim.
- Clark and Brennan, "Grounding in communication" (1991): what was meant is established jointly, by grounding, as part of language use. Ingest in progress.
- Lakoff, "Hedges: a study in meaning criteria and the logic of fuzzy concepts" (1973): the fuzzy-logic treatment of natural-language vagueness. No copy accessible without a login; the fuzziness claim above stays suspended.
- Clark and Brennan, "Grounding in communication" (1991): what was meant is established jointly, by grounding, for the current purpose. Deferred. It would support one sentence of the side theory, that repair settles what the parties take an utterance to mean here rather than what it conventionally means, and the side theory is not load-bearing. Snapshot and connection report exist in ignored caches; draft the ingest if the side theory earns a note. The connection pass flagged that "grounding" has a different sense in this KB, source support for claims.

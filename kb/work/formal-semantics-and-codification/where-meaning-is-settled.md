# Where natural-language meaning is settled

Tentative theory, 2026-11-01, revised after [Astra's review](./review-2026-11-01-astra.md). This file is a side result of the [workshop](./README.md). Nothing in the [commitment account](./codification-as-commitment.md) or the [proposed definitions](./candidate-definitions.md) depends on it. Its claims are weaker than the first draft's, and several are suspended.

## The claim

Natural language has prior constraints but no designated reference. The constraints are conventions a community holds. They make some readings wrong, whether or not the author is available. No artifact or consumer is authoritative over the conventions: they are a population fact, which [meaning as coordination](../../theory/natural-language-meaning-is-coordination-among-learned-approximations.md) states as a hypothesis. When a dispute about a reading arises between parties, it is resolved locally, by repair, and what repair settles is what the parties will take the utterance to mean here, not what it conventionally means.

Three things the first draft ran together must be kept apart: what an expression conventionally means, what its author intended, and what the parties agree to do next. Repair connects them. It does not make them identical. "Meaning is settled by its author on request" was too simple.

## Four features

**Repair.** "I don't understand, explain what you mean" is a channel built into the language, not a failure of it. Conversation analysis treats repair as a structural feature of talk, and the two ingested sources below support that. They also limit it: most clarification requests in dialogue concern what was heard or what move was made, and only a minority ask what an expression means. Repair is frequent and structured; it is not shown to be the usual way meaning gets settled. A compiler cannot ask the programmer what they meant, and Turing's clerk follows the rules with no authority to deviate. The [commitment account](./codification-as-commitment.md) states the narrower consequence: codification removes the channel for asking what the artifact means through a step the rules did not declare, while declared clarification steps about inputs remain.

**Dynamism.** "Semantics is not set in stone" is not the contrast, because programming languages also change. What differs is the mechanism. A designated reference changes only by revision under its authority, and consumers between revisions are held to the old one. Natural language changes through use, with no authority, because local repairs and new uses can shift what a community takes a word to mean.

**Redefinition.** Some programming languages let a program redefine parts of the language: Lisp macros, operator overloading, Forth words, Smalltalk's metaclasses. The redefinition is always inside a fixed reference. It is interpreted by the reference as it stood at that moment, there is a definite current definition in force, and the specification that says what a definition does sits outside the language. Natural language goes deeper in three ways. A redefinition need not be declared: "literally" and "awful" changed through use. A redefinition is a proposal, not an act in force: it takes hold through uptake, and competing meanings coexist across speakers at once. The semantic vocabulary is inside the language: "means", "define" and "true" drift like any other word, so there is no fixed kernel in which definitions are stated. Tarski called this semantic closure.

**Fuzziness.** Vague predicates are settled case by case, and the boundary of the boundary is itself vague. Fuzzy logic, applied to natural-language hedges in the 1970s, did not take hold in linguistics. The first draft explained this by saying that a membership function is a designated reference and speakers reject it. That is a causal and historical claim the workshop has not grounded, and it is suspended until Lakoff's 1973 paper or a history of the fuzzy-semantics programme is ingested. What stands without it is narrower: a membership function would be a designated reference, so formalizing vagueness as degrees converts it into the other form. Whether that is why the programme failed is open.

## Underspecification

The library treats [underspecification](../../theory/agentic-systems-interpret-underspecified-instructions.md), the fact that two readings of an instruction can both be reasonable, as the basic feature agents must cope with. The first draft derived it from repair. That was too strong: missing context and unanticipated cases arise with no expectation of repair. Underspecification has several sources. An author cannot settle what they did not foresee. Drift and redefinition reopen choices the author did settle. Vague predicates leave every boundary open. And because repair is usually available, authors can leave open what repair will settle. Repair explains why underspecification is cheap in ordinary use, not why it exists. The gap becomes consequential when no repair is available, and the commitment account says what follows for an author handing an artifact to a consumer that cannot ask.

The programming-language contrast is that a designated reference declares its gaps in advance, while natural language's gaps are discovered on consumption. This is a claim about what the reference names, not about what a consumer can decide before running a program.

## A conjecture about an LLM as a reference for English

A frozen model designated as a reference for English would be a snapshot of one idiolect with its channel to the community closed. The first draft inferred that English cannot have a reference because it has no state for a reference to capture. Astra's objection stands: variation and change do not establish this, and a model might represent variation, context and change rather than one static dictionary. The conjecture is retained as a conjecture, not a premise, and it is not added to the [probe question](./perfect-english-reference-question.md).

The weaker question remains open: whether a sufficiently competent model could reasonably be designated as a reference for a scoped variety of English. It is a research question about language fidelity, not a classification criterion. The probe question retains it, and the [ideal-interpreter workshop](../ideal-interpreter/README.md) owns the interpreter device.

**Semantic expansion** is a separate question. "Return an integer greater than 10" permits several answers without disagreement about which integers qualify. A different case arises when interpreters disagree about what is permitted and the semantics is revised to settle it, then meets a further disagreement, indefinitely. That no stage is final does not establish that no fixed account of the sequence exists. No impossibility result about natural-language semantics is assumed here, and none is needed: this file does not claim that English lacks a formal semantics, only that it lacks a designated reference.

## Tarski may hold the core of the distinction

The operator's suggestion, 2026-11-01: Tarski's points are subtle, and they may be at the core of the difference rather than a side remark. The [1944 paper](../../sources/tarski-semantic-conception-of-truth.ingest.md) has now been ingested as the primary-source audit. It supports less than the first draft of this section claimed, and it relocates the part that might matter most.

**Exactly specified structure.** Tarski says the problem of truth has a precise meaning only for languages whose structure has been exactly specified: which expressions are meaningful, which terms are undefined, what the rules of definition are, which expressions are sentences, and under what conditions a sentence may be asserted. For natural, spoken languages the problem is vague and its solution can only be approximate. This is the part that sits close to the two conjuncts of the [commitment account](./codification-as-commitment.md): a reference that a clerk could follow presupposes a language whose sentences and assertion conditions a clerk could recognize. It is a precondition, not a result, and Tarski states it as such.

**Semantic closure and the metalanguage.** Three properties must be kept apart here, because the first draft of this thread ran them together. *Formalizability* is exactly specified structure; arithmetic and programming languages have it. *Undefinability* is Tarski's theorem: a consistent exactly specified language that can name its own sentences cannot define its own truth predicate, so truth for it is defined in an essentially richer metalanguage. Arithmetic has this property, and it is a consequence of being formalized and consistent, not a failure of formalization. *The antinomy* hits only a language that is exactly specified, classical, fully disquotational and semantically closed, that is, one that contains its own truth predicate for its own sentences. Arithmetic is not semantically closed, which is why it is consistent. Everyday language appears to be, which is why Tarski guesses that an exactly specified language resembling it would be inconsistent.

The ingest's limits on what this gives the workshop follow. Undefinability holds for arithmetic and for programming languages, so it cannot be what separates them from natural language. The 1944 paper never mentions computation or execution. The defensible core is narrower: natural language cannot be formalized while keeping universality, classical logic and full disquotation together, so one of them must go, and every formalization of a fragment drops universality. A further question the ingest raises for the thread: a programming language can contain its own interpreter, as Lisp does, without contradiction, because an evaluator fixes execution rather than truth and a partial function has no liar. Whether "operationally closed but not semantically closed" is the precise form of the programming-language side is open.

**Universality.** The claim that colloquial language is universal, that whatever can be said can be said in it, so that it absorbs any metalanguage, is in the 1933 monograph, not the 1944 paper. The structural conjecture that depended on it stands or falls with that text, which has not been ingested: a programming language's reference is stated outside it, in a metalanguage of English, mathematics and a machine, while a reference for natural language has nowhere outside to stand except a machine adopted by stipulation or a restriction to a fragment with exactly specified structure. The redefinition feature above, that the semantic vocabulary is inside the language, is this universality seen from the speaker's side. Until the monograph is ingested, this is a conjecture attributed to Tarski by reputation, not by quotation.

**What must not be claimed.** For everyday language Tarski says the consistency question "has no exact meaning", and he only risks the guess that an exactly specified language resembling it would be inconsistent. The library's note on [underspecified instructions](../../theory/agentic-systems-interpret-underspecified-instructions.md) currently appeals to "Tarski, Gödel and halting-style impossibility results" in one breath, and the [institution-theory workshop](../institution-theory-import/import-assessment.md) flagged that appeal for this audit. The ingest's assessment is that the appeal can draw the hierarchy of languages and the absence of a general truth test, and cannot draw a theorem about natural language. That paragraph should be rewritten to the narrower claim once this thread settles, with the four passages the ingest names retained as quotes.

## Sources to ingest

Each supports one named claim. Status as of 2026-11-01:

- [Schegloff, Jefferson and Sacks, "The preference for self-correction in the organization of repair in conversation" (1977)](../../sources/schegloff-jefferson-sacks-self-correction-repair.ingest.md): repair is a structural feature of conversation with its own organization, not a failure mode. Ingested. The paper supports that claim: repair occurs where nothing is hearably wrong, errors often go unrepaired, nothing is in principle excluded from the repairable, and the authors call repair the self-righting mechanism of language use in interaction. It does not separate what an expression conventionally means from what the speaker intended, and it ties repair to continuing the next action. No quotes retained yet. The KB uses "self-repair" for an LLM regenerating code until a check passes; the paper's sense is repair by the speaker, so the collision ledger may need an entry.
- [Purver, Ginzburg and Healey, "On the means for clarification in dialogue" (2001)](../../sources/purver-ginzburg-healey-clarification-dialogue.ingest.md): clarification requests are frequent in ordinary dialogue and have classifiable forms and readings. Open stand-in for Ginzburg's *The Interactive Stance* (2012), which is not freely available. Ingested. It supports frequency and classifiability only: requests are about 3 to 4 percent of dialogue sentences in the British National Corpus, with eight forms and four readings, and 96 percent target a sentence at most four back. Only about 15 percent ask what an expression refers to; most ask whether the words were heard or what move the speaker made. So the repair feature above must not be read as showing that meaning in general is settled by asking. Single annotator, no agreement check. No quotes retained yet.
- [Tarski, "The semantic conception of truth and the foundations of semantics" (1944)](../../sources/tarski-semantic-conception-of-truth.ingest.md): exactly specified structure as the precondition for a precise semantics (section 6); the semantically closed language and the decision not to use one (section 8); truth defined in an essentially richer metalanguage (section 10). Ingested, whole paper captured. Universality of colloquial language is not in this paper; it is in the 1933 monograph, which is not ingested. Undefinability of a language's own truth holds for formalized arithmetic too, and the paper says nothing about computation, so neither separates natural from programming languages; the antinomy itself hits only semantically closed languages, which arithmetic is not. No quotes retained yet; the ingest names four passages to ground.
- Tarski, "The concept of truth in formalized languages" (1933, English 1956): the universality of colloquial language and the guess that it is inconsistent. Not ingested. Needed before the structural conjecture above can be attributed to Tarski by quotation.
- Clark and Brennan, "Grounding in communication" (1991): what was meant is established jointly, by grounding, as part of language use. Ingest in progress.
- Lakoff, "Hedges: a study in meaning criteria and the logic of fuzzy concepts" (1973): the fuzzy-logic treatment of natural-language vagueness. No copy accessible without a login; the fuzziness claim above stays suspended.

# The Bitter Lesson puts external theory retention in question, not theory search

The Bitter Lesson favors search and learning that can make use of more computation over methods that depend on people encoding what they know about a domain. The distinction is who supplies the theory, not whether the theory is written down. A learner that proposes rival explanations, derives their predictions and tests them is using search and learning. Writing down the explanation it selects does not turn that explanation into a human-supplied prior.

The narrower question is whether the learner still needs the written theory as it scales. A larger model might compare explanations and revise its conclusions during inference without ever storing a theory as a separate object. If more computation or a longer context yields the same result, a written theory that helps today may be compensating for a current limit. The Bitter Lesson gives a reason to test that possibility, not a verdict on it.

The test is sharpest where a later decision depends on *which* theory survived an earlier one. Can a new session use the earlier theory without reconstructing it? When a counterexample invalidates one assumption, can the learner narrow that theory without disturbing conclusions that still hold? Can a process other than the one that formed the theory inspect the assumption behind a prediction? Compare those outcomes with and without a separately stored theory as model scale and context increase. Success on the original prediction alone would not show whether external retention still does useful work.

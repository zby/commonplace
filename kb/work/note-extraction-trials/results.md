# The drafts checked whether a theory must be stored, not whether its tests show transfer

Four writers completed the [paired trial](./README.md) (one draft per condition per case). A fifth fresh worker compared the pairs against the original sources without the condition key, later commits or experimental instruction. The comparison questions were supplied, not discovered by that reviewer. No draft was published. This is a comparison of outputs, not an estimate of the instruction's effect.

## A: Two plausible inferences, one examined

Both the [ordinary extraction](./drafts/A1.md) and the [instructed extraction](./drafts/A2.md) correctly separate two questions: a model that proposes and tests theories is doing search; a scaled model might do that without a separately stored theory. A2 explicitly says compatibility with the Bitter Lesson “does not establish that the method will scale well.” It uses the source’s build example to show that a correct decision reveals neither whether the model retrieved a stored rule nor whether the correction will persist. A1 makes an equally useful distinction: can the next session use the theory “without reconstructing it”? A2 asking whether the correction still guides the session leaves reconstruction possible. Neither draft wins on every distinction.

Neither states the more consequential qualification the historical extraction made in `7b1620219`: a search can accept a theory that fits every source observation without testing whether it transfers. That matters to the parent’s argument about sample efficiency *after a shift*. The writers lacked the historical edit's external evidence, but not the conceptual contrast: “What would test the conjecture” in the pinned source explicitly contrasts a preserved mechanism with a deceptive correlation that breaks after a shift. The missing step was connecting that distinction to the shared passage. Both citation sets instead send the reader to the open retention question.

## B: A settled argument survived either way

Both the [instructed extraction](./drafts/B1.md) and the [ordinary extraction](./drafts/B2.md) locate partial evaluation in the whole revised prompt, not the inserted listing. Both preserve the limit: specializing an instruction to known inputs does not guarantee identical model answers. B1 is leaner; B2 retains the source equation, names binding-time analysis, and preserves reliability as well as context as a benefit. Neither adds a spurious objection. B1’s citation wording is sound, but its draft-relative link would not resolve if pasted into the historical parent. The drafts are retained as written. No inference about time or effort follows from the prose alone.

## What to try next

Keep the instruction opt-in. Its contrast can be apt but aimed at a side question. A candidate change for a **different** case: *Write the sentence in which the parent will use the note. What could a reader wrongly infer from that sentence even if the note’s literal claim were true? Test that inference first.* Skip this when no parent relies on the note as a premise. Compare that variant to the current instruction on a new case rather than teaching writers the historical answer and repeating A. No instruction change or adoption decision was made in this trial.

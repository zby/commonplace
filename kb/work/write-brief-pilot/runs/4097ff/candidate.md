# A context-operation interface bounds the projections its policy can realize

Retained state and active context are different runtime layers. Here, the retained substrate is projectable state available outside a bounded model input; retained controller state is tracked separately when it selects projections but is not itself projected into that input. Active context is what one bounded invocation actually receives. This follows the broader separation between [state retention and context assembly](./agent-runtime-analysis-should-separate-scheduling-context-state.md). A **context-operation interface** is the operations and permitted compositions through which a controller locates, materializes, transforms, and exposes retained state as active context. Its addressable units and exposure boundary are part of the interface. A controller or context policy chooses whether, when, and how to use that interface; it may be the receiving model, a learned controller, a host or proxy, or a mixed arrangement. Holding the retained substrate, model, and resource budget fixed, the interface bounds the projections that policy can realize.

Let `I` include the operation semantics, addressable units, composition rules, and the boundary where a view becomes model input. For retained state `S`, task and run signal `x`, fixed model or models `M`, and fixed budget `B`, define `Reach(I, S, x; M, B)` as the active-context views obtainable through legal interface traces. `B` includes the applicable active-context, model-call, time, and tool-use limits. A trace may locate, expand, transform, summarize, filter, delete, or expose retained material. It may also update retained state before exposure. When an operation itself calls a model, as a summarizer does, its result can vary between runs; `Reach` then contains every view some legal trace can produce. "Projection" therefore means the task- and state-conditioned view delivered to the model, not a lossless or non-mutating mathematical projector. A required view lies outside `Reach` if producing it needs an unavailable operation, an unaddressable distinction, or a forbidden composition. In particular, [access and transformation impose distinct burdens](./access-burden-and-transformation-burden-are-distinct-query-dimensions.md): finding retained input does not imply that the interface can turn it into the view the call needs.

A policy selects legal traces, so it induces achieved coverage or a distribution within structural reach. A better policy may choose useful traces more often, sequence them more reliably, or reduce their cost. Those gains do not add a view outside `Reach`, nor do they show that excluded projections are unnecessary. This is the context-projection instance of the more general result that [learning inside a fixed decomposition inherits its omissions](./learning-inside-a-fixed-decomposition-inherits-its-mistakes.md). If an intervention adds observations, external computation, tools, model capability, budget, or permission to change operation definitions, then a premise has changed. It is no longer only a better policy over the same interface.

## The interface as the controller's action space

For a controller that chooses context operations, the context-operation interface is its action space together with the constraints on which action sequences are legal. The operations are the actions, and the addressable units are the arguments those actions can take. The composition rules act as an action mask over sequences. The operation semantics are the transition function on retained state. The exposure boundary fixes which results enter the receiving model's input. A legal trace is a trajectory this action space permits, and `B` plays the role of the horizon and resource limits on an episode.

In these terms, the claim is a statement about reachable sets. Any policy over the action space, whether trained, prompted, or hand-written, puts its probability only on trajectories that the action space permits. Optimizing the policy, by reinforcement learning, distillation, or prompt revision, moves probability mass among those trajectories. It does not add trajectories. A policy that is optimal for one action space is optimal relative to that action space, and its return says nothing about whether a different action set would reach a better view. Changing the action set, the observation the controller receives, the transition semantics, or the horizon changes the decision problem itself. Such a change can raise the attainable return, but it can also make learning harder, because more actions raise exploration and credit-assignment cost. That trade-off is why a restricted interface can be the better choice even when its reach is smaller.

Two further separations carry over. Retained controller state, such as learned policy weights or a stored invocation rule, is part of the policy and is not part of the retained substrate the actions operate on. Whether a policy can be trained at all depends on the feedback signal, which is a separate question: [feedback-trained memory management needs an oracle even when its operations are hand-designed](./memory-management-policy-is-learnable-but-oracle-dependent.md).

## Three systems side by side

Three source-described systems build bounded model context through different action spaces. A [practitioner account of recursive language models](../sources/recursive-language-models-what-finally-gave-me-the-aha-moment.ingest.md) (RLM) describes model-authored programmatic exploration and transformation. The paper-described [lambda-RLM](../sources/the-y-combinator-for-llms-solving-long-context-rot.ingest.md) replaces arbitrary programs with a small typed combinator language. Paper-described [agentic context management](../sources/acm-agentic-context-management-for-long-horizon-tasks.ingest.md) (ACM) fixes two memory operations and trains when to invoke or abstain from them.

| | RLM (practitioner account) | lambda-RLM (preprint) | ACM (preprint) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Controller and how its policy is set | The receiving model, prompted | The model picks a task type from a fixed menu; a deterministic planner sets branching factor, leaf threshold, and composition operator | The agent model, post-trained by teacher-guided on-policy distillation |
| Action space (context operations) | Arbitrary Python over a `context` variable in a persistent REPL, plus recursive sub-model calls | Typed combinators such as `SPLIT`, `MAP`, `FILTER`, `REDUCE`, and `PEEK`; model calls only at bounded leaves | `manage_context` (summarize a prefix, archive its raw messages under an identifier) and `query_memory` (retrieve from the archive), beside search and answer actions |
| Composition and exposure | Any program the sandbox runs; views reach the model as truncated printed output, as REPL variables, or as a returned variable | One combinator chain, fixed before execution | Summary boundaries follow chronology; recall is pull-only, and a querier model selects what returns |
| What the source reports about how well it works | No measurement; one letter-counting walkthrough | Accuracy wins over standard RLM in 29 of 36 comparisons; 3-6x lower latency among recursive methods | Pass@1 gains on three benchmarks from adding the operations and again from training |
| What the comparison varies | Nothing | Action space and controller together | First the action space, then only the policy |

What each source reports:

- **RLM.** The account reports no controlled measurement. It walks through one task, counting a letter across 50 fruit names, in which direct generation, RAG, ReAct, and CodeAct fail or scale poorly, and presents RLM as the architecture that handles it.
- **lambda-RLM.** Across four long-context tasks and nine open-weight models, the paper reports that lambda-RLM beats standard RLM in 29 of 36 model-task accuracy comparisons, with 3-6x latency reductions among recursive methods. The wins concentrate on weaker models: lambda-RLM wins 6 of 12 comparisons on the strongest tier, and standard RLM wins most often on CodeQA, where free-form repository navigation matters.
- **ACM.** On Qwen3.5-9B, adding the two operations to a ReAct agent raises pass@1 from 0.570 to 0.635 on BrowseComp-Plus, from 0.367 to 0.405 on DeepSearchQA, and from 0.489 to 0.508 on SWE-Bench Verified. Post-training the policy over the same operations raises these to 0.727, 0.425, and 0.530. Peak active context generally falls while tool calls often rise. A 4B model that stops after about two turns scores 3.4%, so the operations never come into play.

The numbers are not comparable across columns, because tasks, models, and baselines differ. Read through the interface/policy split, they show different things. ACM's second step is a policy improvement inside a fixed action space, which is the case this note says cannot establish interface adequacy; its first step changes the action space but compares it only with no context management, not with a rival operation set. lambda-RLM against standard RLM is the only rival-interface comparison of the three, but it changes the action space and the controller at once, so its result belongs to the pair and cannot be assigned to either. Its CodeQA losses fit the reading that some needed views lie outside the combinator library's reach, but the paper does not isolate that cause. The RLM account varies nothing and measures nothing. These sources illustrate the variable; the note's claim was abstracted from them, and their reports do not test it.

Who selects a projection is a separate coordinate from what the action space contains. In the RLM account the receiving model selects its own view; in lambda-RLM a planner fixes the program after one model choice; in ACM a trained agent chooses among fixed operations. A host or proxy can instead assemble a view before the receiving model sees it, while a mixed push/pull arrangement can combine preloaded material with model-requested expansion. These placements can coexist with different action spaces, so controller placement does not by itself determine structural reach. Within-run, cross-restart, and cross-task retained state instead describe how long prior state can condition selection. Changing a retained invocation policy is not by itself a change to operation semantics or composition rules.

## Evaluation consequence

An evaluation of controller improvement should report the retained substrate and addressable units; operation semantics and composition rules; controller placement and projection boundary; mutable policies or artifacts and their persistence horizon; model; and resource budget. It should identify which coordinates stayed fixed and which changed. In action-space terms, a benchmark gain from a better policy is a policy comparison under one action space. Gains with one fixed operation set show that a policy was useful in the tested regime. They do not establish that excluded operations are unnecessary. Testing interface adequacy requires a rival operation/composition interface, ideally varied while the controller is held fixed, another constraint-changing intervention, or an argument that projections excluded by the interface cannot improve the objective.

## Scope

Structural reach is not achieved use. Exact retained material can coexist with a lossy or missed active projection. A legal trace may never be discovered or produced reliably, and a delivered view may still fail to affect behavior. The claim here ends at exposure into active context; [storage, read-back, and contextual activation remain distinct](./knowledge-storage-does-not-imply-contextual-activation.md).

The claim does not rank interfaces. A restricted interface may trade admitted transformations for reliability, safety, inspectability, trainability, latency, or cost. An open-ended programming interface remains bounded by its primitives, sandbox, permitted compositions, model competence, and budget. The system contrasts show that these architecture coordinates vary across described systems. The reported results belong to each source's own setup; they do not estimate the causal performance effect of changing any one coordinate, and they leave open which interface is preferable under a given set of operational constraints.

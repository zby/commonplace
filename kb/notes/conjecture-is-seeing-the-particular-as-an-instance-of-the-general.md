# Unifying conjecture makes a general and its instances intelligible together

A **unifying conjecture** proposes a general structure and recasts previously known particulars as instances of it. It is one inferential form within conjecture — the phase of the [discovery lifecycle](./definitions/discovery-lifecycle.md) in which someone posits a claim not entailed by the evidence. Unlike a singular hypothesis, it does not merely posit an entity or cause for one case. Unlike a prediction, it does not merely extend an existing theory to an unseen case. It changes how known cases are understood together.

The structure need not be new to the field. It need only be new to the investigator's representation of the problem, or to a knowledge base in which the cases were previously represented separately. The cases constrain the general's content and scope; the general identifies what matters about the cases and how they relate. Each side therefore makes the other more intelligible.

## Three forms of unification

Unifying conjectures can relate cases through three forms of increasing explanatory commitment:

| Form | Operation | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| **Shared feature** | Group cases by an observable similarity | Organizes the cases without explaining the similarity |
| **Shared structure** | Extract a reusable relation, pattern, or proof shape | Reveals a common form across different contexts |
| **Generative model** | Propose a process capable of producing the cases | Explains their commonality and may predict new consequences |

The forms are not strictly nested. A generative model may unite cases that look different on the surface, while similar-looking cases may have different causes. Greater explanatory commitment also creates more ways to be wrong: a feature may be misclassified, a shared structure may be spurious, and a generative model may fit the observed cases yet fail on new ones.

## A witness and a boundary

Mathematical lemma extraction is a clean witness. A mathematician notices that two proofs make the same move and states that move as a lemma: a reusable intermediate result. The lemma becomes a new general in the local proof vocabulary, and the earlier proofs can be redescribed as instances that apply it. Before this abstraction, the proofs are already known, but their common structure is not available as a premise for later work.

A singular causal hypothesis marks the boundary. Inferring that one contaminant caused one failed experiment is conjectural when the evidence does not entail that cause. Yet the inference need not create a general or regroup known cases. It belongs to the broader conjecture phase without performing the unifying operation described here.

## Knowledge-base consequence

This account separates retrieving potentially related cases from recognizing what unifies them. Recognition cost depends on the reader's prior vocabulary, tools, and access to the relevant cases. Similarity search over numerical representations can cheaply surface candidates that share features. Recognizing a shared argument or proof structure usually requires semantic comparison. A generative model requires a further step: proposing a process and testing whether it explains more than the cases that suggested it.

Once a structure has a name and usable criteria for recognizing its instances, later comparisons can become cheaper. Naming amortizes discovery cost only when the structure is reusable and its criteria exclude tempting false positives. In a knowledge base, a new mechanism note earns its place when this expected reuse and discrimination value exceed the context cost of adding another abstraction.

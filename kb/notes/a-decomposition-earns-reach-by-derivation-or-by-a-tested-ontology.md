# A decomposition earns reach by derivation or by a tested ontology; the rest is free choice

A decomposition claims more than it displays. The categories are visible; the assertion that they will keep cutting the domain in the right places — in cases the designer has not seen — is not. That assertion is a [reach](./first-principles-reasoning-selects-for-explanatory-reach-over.md) claim, and since [use tests a decomposition only locally](./use-tests-a-decomposition-locally-rationale-makes-transfer-testable.md), running the system with the carve in it does not touch it.

There are exactly three places the claim can come from, and they are distinguished by what the designer did *not* choose.

**Derivation from inherited constraints.** The categories fall out of constraints the designer had no say over. What is chosen is the axes; once the axes are fixed the cells are forced, including the empty ones. Reach is then inherited: the carve holds exactly as far as the constraints that generated it hold, and the boundary is statable in advance rather than discovered by failure.

**Inheritance of a tested ontology.** Someone else's carve, already exposed to cases. Repeated use across varied contexts is the extensional route to a transfer claim — real, and priced at one full rebuild per sample. Borrowing a mature ontology is buying that sample secondhand: decades of use, paid for by another field. The purchase is genuine and the discount is large.

**Free choice.** Underdetermined by anything, settled by convenience or by what was in the author's head. It supports no reach claim at all, and its only obligation is to stay cheap to replace.

## The provenance triple is the rationale triple, raised from boundaries to the carve

Retained rationale records, per boundary, whether it answers an **inherited constraint**, a **local requirement**, or a **free choice**. The three provenances are those same categories asked about the carve as a whole: derivation is the inherited-constraint case; free choice is unchanged; and the middle case is where inheriting an ontology sits — and sits uncomfortably.

That is the cost hidden in the secondhand purchase. You acquire the split without its per-boundary rationale. The source's inherited constraints and its local requirements arrive bundled and indistinguishable, so the borrowed carve looks uniformly load-bearing when part of it was only ever answering conditions in the source domain. The sample you bought was drawn under those conditions. It licenses the carve here only while the constraints that made it carve *there* still hold — and telling which those were is work the borrowing does not come with.

This is why an ontology and a technique face different bars. [Adoption bars set by source field](./programming-patterns-get-a-fast-pass-but-other-borrowed-ideas-must.md) ask whether the borrowing domain is mechanistically close enough for its methods to transfer. A technique can settle that by working. An ontology cannot: it transfers by continuing to cut at places that matter, which is a claim about the target domain's structure, not about the technique's fit. Closeness of fields is evidence for it and not a substitute.

## What the routes are routes to

[What scale replaces is a generalization whose scope was asserted rather than tested](./bitter-lesson-selects-against-unearned-reach-not-against-structure.md) — and a carve is a generalization, asserting that these seams will keep mattering. The two routes are the two ways that scope gets earned instead of asserted: derivation earns it intensionally, from constraints whose own scope is known; inheritance earns it extensionally, from cases someone already ran. A carve with neither provenance is not a worse carve — it may well be right — but its scope was asserted from the fit of the cases that produced it, which is the exact property that loses.

So the free-choice category is not a confession of sloppiness. It is the accurate label for most carves most of the time, and labelling it correctly is what keeps it cheap. The failure mode is not picking a free choice; it is letting one harden. A free choice that has been encoded into a directory layout, a schema field, or a vocabulary term that a hundred artifacts cite has acquired revision cost without ever acquiring reach — it is expensive to change and there was never a reason it was right.

## Two instances, one system, both provenances

[Representational form](./definitions/representational-form.md) takes the derived route. Its three categories are generated by two axes — whether a defined consumer assigns consequences to the artifact, and whether there is a localized unit carrying the content. The derivation earns two things use could not. The boundary is stated ahead of the failure: the fourth cell is empty in this domain rather than impossible, so the three-way carve holds while agent systems retain nothing of vector-symbolic shape and gains a form if they do. And the downstream rule falls out instead of being stipulated — read, test, probe follow from the axes, which is what a derived carve buys that a list of three categories with three attached rules does not.

[Reflective system](./definitions/reflective-system.md) takes the inherited route. Causal connection, self-representation, and theory-relativity come from Maes 1988 with the Smith 1984 lineage behind them, carried as `derived-from` edges to the source ingests. What makes this a disciplined inheritance rather than an appeal to authority is that the definition separates the purchase from the extensions: a provenance section lists what was inherited, and names retrieval-as-causal-connection as Commonplace's own with no source behind it. The bought sample covers interpreters and metaobject protocols, where the causal wire is exhaustive. Whether the criteria still carve when the wire is best-effort discovery is outside what was purchased, so the note has to argue it separately — and does, in its own section.

## Scope

- Provenance is not correctness. A derived carve can be derived from the wrong axes, and an inherited one can come from an ontology that was well tested against questions nobody asks any more. The claim is about what the scope assertion rests on, not about whether the categories are good.
- The routes are not exclusive. Re-deriving a borrowed carve from local constraints is the strongest position available, and the most expensive; it is also hard to distinguish from rationalizing the borrowed split after the fact, which inherits the [worse-than-none asymmetry](./use-tests-a-decomposition-locally-rationale-makes-transfer-testable.md) that unfaithful rationale carries.
- **"Tested" is the weak joint.** Nothing here supplies a criterion for it, and longevity is a bad proxy: an ontology can survive because it was never probed, which is the same failure as a reach claim that no test could have refuted. The purchase is only as good as the testing behind it, and the buyer usually cannot audit that.
- **Commonplace has not demonstrated that this transfers.** Two instances, authored inside the system that states the claim, assessed by nobody outside it. What they establish is that both provenances are recordable and that the record makes departures auditable — not that recording them produces better carves, and not that the discipline survives contact with a designer who wants the carve they already had.

## Open Questions

- What would make "already tested" checkable by a borrower who cannot re-run the source field's cases — is there anything short of finding a case that should have broken the original carve and did not?
- Whether a re-derivation of a borrowed carve can be separated from a rationalization of it by any means other than intervention on the stated constraint.
- Whether "stay replaceable" has an operational form — a position a free-choice carve must not occupy, a cost ceiling — or whether it stays advice that loses to the first artifact that cites it.
